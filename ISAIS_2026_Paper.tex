{\Large\textbf{ARS-VG: A Graph-Theoretic Intelligent Artifact for Joint
Detection of Accrual-Based and Real-Activities Earnings Management
Substitution in SEC-Registered Firms: A Design Science Research
Approach}}
\end{center}

% --- Abstract ----------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Although the substitution between accrual-based earnings management (AEM)
and real activities manipulation (REM) is well documented at the aggregate
level, existing detection tools analyze each channel in isolation, leaving
firm-level substitution unobservable.  This paper presents
\textbf{ARS-VG} (Accrual--Real Substitution Vulnerability Graph), a Design
Science Research artifact that models financial statements as directed
weighted graphs, applies relative $z$-score anomaly detection,
and incorporates governance constraints to classify substitution.
Evaluated on the JarFraud benchmark (146{,}045 observations) against the
Beneish M-Score, Dechow F-Score, and \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost (a
random-undersampling boosted-tree ensemble), the
ensemble achieves the highest recall (77.1 percent) at the Youden-optimal
threshold; RUSBoost dominates at strict operating points.  A
feature-asymmetry decomposition attributes roughly three-quarters of
RUSBoost's discrimination advantage (the area under the ROC curve, AUC)
to richer raw-feature access rather
than non-linear learning, pricing an interpretability-versus-discrimination
tradeoff at $0.194$ AUC.  Scenario
testing verifies correct rule-execution across four theoretically
calibrated manipulation regimes, including a governance-moderated
AEM$\to$REM case.  Population-scale evaluation of the
governance-moderated classifier, by contrast, does not
succeed under the panel's covariate-sparse governance
specification; we report this transparently and characterize the
mechanism, which generalizes to other discrete,
threshold-gated governance classifiers.
ARS-VG is positioned as observational research infrastructure and a
demonstrated proof-of-concept, not an audit
screen or a population-validated detector.
\end{abstract}

\section*{\centering I. INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Financial statement fraud remains one of the most costly and persistent
challenges in capital markets.  The Association of Certified Fraud
Examiners \citep{acfe2024} estimates that organizations lose approximately
5 percent of annual revenue to occupational fraud broadly defined, with
financial statement schemes, though less frequent than asset
misappropriation, producing the largest median losses per incident.  Despite
advances in auditing standards and regulatory oversight, high-profile
scandals, from Enron and WorldCom to more recent cases such as
Wirecard, underscore the difficulty of detecting earnings manipulation in a
timely manner.

Academic research has responded with a rich portfolio of statistical models.
The Beneish M-Score \citep{beneish1999} uses financial-ratio analysis to flag
firms likely to have manipulated earnings through accrual-based techniques.
The Dechow F-Score \citep{dechow2011} incorporates accrual quality measures
derived from the Richardson et al.\ \citep{richardson2005} RSST accrual
decomposition, soft-asset ratios, and changes in receivables.  More recently,
machine learning methods (random forests, gradient boosting, neural
networks) have been applied to fraud detection with promising results
\citep{bao2020,cecchini2010,craja2020}.  However, these data-driven
approaches often function as ``black boxes,'' producing predictions without the
interpretable audit trail that practitioners require \citep{green1997}.

A critical gap in all these approaches is their treatment of financial accounts
as \emph{independent} variables.  In practice, financial statements are a
system of interconnected accounts governed by double-entry bookkeeping,
industry-specific operating cycles, and accrual accounting rules.  When a firm
manipulates earnings, the manipulation rarely distorts a single ratio in
isolation; instead, it creates \emph{strain} that propagates across related
accounts \citep{dechow1995}.  Revenue inflation through premature recognition
simultaneously distorts the revenue-to-receivables relationship (elevated days
sales outstanding), the revenue-to-cash-flow relationship (diverging operating
cash flows), and the accrual-to-income relationship (increasing discretionary
accruals).  Traditional methods may detect each symptom separately, but they
do not model the \emph{relational structure} that connects them.

This relational perspective is especially important in light of the
well-documented phenomenon of \emph{AEM--REM substitution}.
\citet{cohen2008} and \citet{zang2012} demonstrate that firms strategically
substitute between accrual-based earnings management and real activities
manipulation in response to varying governance constraints: audit quality,
litigation risk, institutional ownership, and regulatory scrutiny.  When
accrual manipulation becomes costly (e.g., after the Sarbanes--Oxley Act of
2002), firms shift toward real activities such as overproduction, discretionary
expense cuts, or channel stuffing \citep{roychowdhury2006}.  This substitution
is an inherently relational phenomenon: pressure that cannot flow through one
manipulation channel is redirected to another.  A detection system that
analyzes each channel independently will miss the substitution pattern.

This motivates our central research question:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} How can firm-level AEM--REM substitution be
operationalized and observed using a graph-theoretic artifact, and how
does such an approach compare to established detection methods in
terms of recall, precision, and interpretability?
\end{quote}

We address this question through the \emph{Design Science Research} (DSR)
paradigm \citep{hevner2004,peffers2007}.  The primary objective of this study
is to design and evaluate a novel artifact, not to test empirical hypotheses
in the traditional sense.  The empirical evaluation that follows serves as
the DSR evaluation mechanism, demonstrating the artifact's utility relative
to established baselines rather than claiming statistical generalization
about fraud incidence.

In this paper, we propose \textbf{ARS-VG}, a graph-theoretic intelligent
artifact developed through the DSR paradigm to address these limitations.  ARS-VG is not
merely a detection tool; it provides an \emph{analytical framework} that
enables researchers and practitioners to observe, measure, and classify
AEM--REM substitution at the individual-firm level, a capability that
existing aggregate-level approaches lack.  Our approach makes six
contributions, four theoretical (TC) and two practical (PC), all of
which are supported by the deterministic graph and ensemble layers
of the artifact.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{TC1}] \textbf{Firm-level substitution framework
    (demonstrated on scenario cases).}  ARS-VG
    operationalizes the AEM--REM substitution hypothesis
    \citep{zang2012,cohen2008} as a firm-level scoring and classification
    system.  By jointly modeling both channels in a single vulnerability
    graph, the artifact enables a proxy-based firm-level classification
    of substitution patterns, demonstrated on four theoretically
    calibrated scenarios (Section~V).  We had intended this
    to extend to population-scale empirical analysis of
    substitution behavior that aggregate cross-sectional designs can only
    infer indirectly (responding to the measurement concerns raised by
    \citet{cohenlys2022} and the evidence of time-varying substitution
    documented by \citet{pincus2022}), but Section~V shows this
    extension is blocked by the public benchmark's governance-covariate
    constraints, and characterizes precisely why.

    \item[\textbf{TC2}] \textbf{Relational view of earnings management
    detection.}  Financial statements are modeled as directed graphs;
    deviations are quantified as \emph{edge strain} using relative
    $z$-scores (industry-adjusted where SIC data is available, pooled
    otherwise; Section~IV).  This relational perspective extends the earnings management
    detection literature beyond the ratio-decomposition paradigm, revealing
    how manipulation creates \emph{strain propagation} across connected
    accounts: a pattern invisible to methods that treat accounts as
    independent variables.

    \item[\textbf{TC3}] \textbf{Governance-moderated substitution:
    architecturally integrated; empirically unreachable under the
    tested specification.}
    The substitution classifier integrates four governance constraints
    (auditor type, institutional ownership, board independence, and
    SOX compliance) into the substitution-classification stage,
    operationalizing the agency-theoretic prediction \citep{jensen1976}
    that strong governance suppresses AEM while redirecting
    manipulation pressure to REM.  All four constraints are exercised
    correctly in the scenario tests (Section~V), a rule-execution
    check rather than an independent construct-validity test, including
    the governance-triggered balloon-effect case.  Applying the SOX arm at
    panel scale on JarFraud (Section~V), however, shows the
    classifier's decision rule to be arithmetically unreachable given
    JarFraud's necessarily fixed values for the remaining three
    constraints: a precise, verified mechanism rather than a weak
    empirical signal.  A full panel-scale test of TC3 would require a
    Compustat/BoardEx merge, which we identify as a natural extension
    and do not claim in the present paper.

    \item[\textbf{TC4}] \textbf{Quantified feature-aggregation cost of
    interpretability.}  By comparing a 28-feature gradient-boosted baseline
    \citep{bao2020} to the same algorithm restricted to ARS-VG's three
    channel-level aggregates, we decompose the aggregate AUC gap into a
    feature-access component ($+0.194$) and an algorithm-class component
    ($+0.065$).  This gives auditor-facing applications, in which
    channel-level decomposition is a requirement rather than a preference,
    a priceable tradeoff between raw-feature discrimination and
    interpretable aggregation.

    \item[\textbf{PC1}] \textbf{Research infrastructure for
    population-scale substitution analysis (contingent on richer
    governance data).}  The intended practical value of
    ARS-VG is as an observational tool for researchers studying
    AEM--REM substitution at the population level, not as a
    production fraud screen.  Realizing this value requires the
    substitution flag to vary meaningfully across the panel; Section~V
    reports that it does not under JarFraud's governance-covariate
    constraints, and notes that a Compustat/BoardEx merge is what
    would be required for this capability to be delivered.  It is
    intended, in any case,
    to be an observational instrument rather than an audit-team triage
    tool: at the Youden operating point, 0.8 percent precision implies
    flagging on the order of ninety thousand firm-years to capture
    seven hundred true positives, which no audit team can follow up.
    At strict low-flag-rate operating points \citet{bao2020}'s
    RUSBoost dominates, and for audit-team deployment we recommend
    Bao's artifact over ours.

    \item[\textbf{PC2}] \textbf{Regulatory monitoring.}  SEC EDGAR integration
    enables scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant
    population without requiring proprietary data access.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~II develops the theoretical foundations.
Section~III describes the DSR methodology.
Section~IV presents the system design.
Section~V reports the empirical evaluation.
Section~VI discusses findings and contributions.
Section~VII concludes.

\section*{\centering II. THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:theory}
% =============================================================================

\subsection*{Agency Theory and Information Asymmetry}

The conceptual anchor of this study is agency theory
\citep{jensen1976,eisenhardt1989}, which models the corporation as a nexus of
contracts between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers).  Because
managers possess superior information about firm prospects and activities, they
can exploit this asymmetry to pursue self-interest, including meeting earnings
benchmarks to preserve compensation or job security
\citep{graham2005,healy1999}.  Governance mechanisms such as Big~4 audit
quality, board independence, institutional ownership, and analyst coverage are
theorized to reduce agency costs by constraining opportunistic reporting
\citep{shleifer1997,watts1986}.

Positive Accounting Theory \citep{watts1986} extends this framework by
predicting specific directions of earnings management: managers adopt
income-increasing strategies when approaching debt-covenant thresholds, when
bonus-plan targets are within reach, and when political scrutiny is low.  These
incentive structures interact with detection risk to determine whether a manager
will choose AEM, REM, or a combination of both \citep{graham2005}.

\subsection*{Accrual-Based Earnings Management}

AEM exploits discretion in accrual accounting to alter reported earnings
without changing underlying cash flows.  The seminal Jones \citeyearpar{jones1991}
model decomposes total accruals into normal (driven by operating conditions) and
discretionary components, with the latter serving as the manipulation proxy.
Subsequent refinements, including the modified Jones model \citep{dechow1995}
and the performance-matched approach \citep{kothari2005}, improved specificity by
controlling for firm performance.  Key AEM indicators include abnormal
accounts-receivable growth, inventory build-up, and divergence between net
income and operating cash flow \citep{richardson2005}.

\subsection*{Real Activities Manipulation}

REM alters reported earnings through actions that affect actual cash flows and
operations.  \citet{roychowdhury2006} identified three primary REM channels:
(i)~abnormally high production to spread fixed costs and reduce cost of goods
sold, resulting in inflated inventory; (ii)~sales acceleration through channel
stuffing or price discounts, reducing cash flow from operations relative to
sales; and (iii)~cuts in discretionary expenditures such as R\&D, SG\&A, and
advertising.  \citet{gunny2010} demonstrates that firms engaging in REM to
just meet earnings benchmarks exhibit lower future operating performance,
confirming that REM imposes real economic costs.

\subsection*{The AEM--REM Substitution Hypothesis}

The most theoretically important development for the present work is the
documented substitution between AEM and REM.  \citet{cohen2008} documented a
structural break after SOX: AEM declined sharply while REM increased,
consistent with managers switching to harder-to-detect manipulation.
\citet{zang2012} formalized this as a sequential decision model: managers
choose REM first (because it is decided during the fiscal year) and use AEM as
a residual to achieve earnings targets, but only to the extent that audit
quality permits.  \citet{cohen2010} replicated the pattern around seasoned
equity offerings, while \citet{ho2015} and \citet{chi2011} showed that Big~4
audit quality suppresses AEM but increases REM.

More recently, \citet{pincus2022} re-examined the substitution effect over an
extended post-SOX period and found that the REM--AEM substitution pattern
weakens in later years, suggesting that the substitution dynamic may be
time-varying rather than monotonic.  \citet{cohenlys2022} provided further
commentary, questioning whether aggregate-level substitution measures
adequately capture firm-level strategic behavior.  These findings do not
invalidate the substitution hypothesis; rather, they motivate a
\emph{firm-level} detection approach, such as ARS-VG, that can identify
substitution at the individual observation level rather than relying on
aggregate cross-sectional trends.

These findings imply that any single-channel detection approach will produce
systematically biased risk scores for firms under high governance pressure.  A
joint detection artifact that captures the \emph{relationship} between AEM and
REM signals is therefore both theoretically motivated and practically necessary.

\subsection*{Graph Analytics in Accounting Information Systems}

Graph-based methods have gained traction in financial applications, primarily
in money laundering detection \citep{savage2014}, insider trading networks
\citep{ahern2017}, and supply chain risk analysis.  More recently, graph neural
networks (GNNs) have been applied to fraud detection in transaction networks
\citep{liu2020,wang2019}.  \citet{caixie2024} proposed a two-layer knowledge graph (FSFD-TLKG) for
financial statement fraud detection, combining semantic and syntactic layers
to model relationships between financial variables.  While FSFD-TLKG also
operates at the firm level, it focuses on \emph{general} fraud pattern mining
without explicitly modeling the AEM--REM substitution dynamic or
incorporating governance constraints as a moderating mechanism.
Concurrently, large language models (LLMs) augmented with
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) have entered the auditing domain:
\citet{jetaaiagent2025} recently demonstrated an AI agent framework in JETA
that integrates RAG with audit methodologies.  However, these approaches
either mine general fraud patterns without theoretical grounding in the
substitution hypothesis, or apply AI to general audit workflows without
modeling specific manipulation channels.  Our vulnerability graph is,
to our knowledge, among the first to apply graph analytics to the
\emph{internal structure} of financial statements for earnings manipulation
detection, operationalizing the relational view of accounting
\citep{dechow2011}.

This study is positioned as a DSR investigation.  We adopt the methodology
of \citet{hevner2004}, operationalized through the six-step process model of
\citet{peffers2007}.  DSR is the appropriate paradigm because the research
objective is to construct and evaluate a novel \emph{artifact}, ARS-VG, whose
utility is demonstrated through rigorous instantiation, not merely inferred
from theory \citep{march1995}.  Accordingly, the empirical analyses in
Section~V serve as the \emph{evaluation} component of the DSR cycle, assessing
whether the artifact fulfills its design objectives.  The contribution lies in
the artifact itself and the theoretical insights it enables, not in the
predictive performance metrics per se.  The three core DSR activities (build,
evaluate, theorize) connect the knowledge base of financial accounting and
information systems to the organizational problem of earnings manipulation
detection.

\subsection*{Problem Identification and Motivation}

As established in Section~II, no existing automated tool jointly
models AEM and REM or detects substitution patterns as filings become
available.  The
economic significance of this gap is confirmed by regulatory enforcement data:
the SEC's AAER database documents over 270 material-misstatement cases between
1982 and 2005, with earnings manipulation the predominant allegation
\citep{dechow2011}.  Existing tools address either relational modeling or
explainability, but none addresses both simultaneously: a design gap that
ARS-VG is specifically engineered to fill.

\subsection*{Objectives of a Solution}

The artifact must satisfy five design objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(O1)] Detect AEM signals with recall at least comparable to
    parametric baselines at the Youden-optimal operating point, and
    occupy a complementary region of the precision--recall frontier
    relative to gradient-boosted ML baselines.
    \item[(O2)] Detect REM signals using production and cash-flow-based anomalies.
    \item[(O3)] Identify and classify AEM--REM substitution patterns.
    \item[(O4)] Expose the underlying edge-level anomalies and
    classification rules in human-readable form, so that each flag can
    be traced to its deterministic components.
    \item[(O5)] Allow interactive querying by CIK number via the SEC EDGAR API.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Design and Development}

ARS-VG was implemented in Python~3.11, leveraging NetworkX for graph
construction and Gradio for the interactive interface.  Section~IV details
the design decisions.

\subsection*{Demonstration}

The artifact was demonstrated on four synthetic but theoretically calibrated
scenarios (Section~V): a clean firm, an AEM-dominant
manipulator, an REM-dominant manipulator, and a substitution case with strong
governance.  All four scenarios produced the expected classification outcome
(pass rate = 100 percent), satisfying design objectives O1--O3.  Objective
O4 (edge-level human-readable exposure of flags) is implemented
natively in the deterministic graph and ensemble layers.  Objective O5 (SEC EDGAR querying) is
implemented in the companion notebook with formal evaluation scoped
to a future practitioner-facing study.

\subsection*{Evaluation}

Utility was evaluated through: (i)~predictive accuracy on the JarFraud
panel under 5-fold stratified cross-validation
(Section~V); (ii)~comparison with four baselines: the
Beneish M-Score, Dechow F-Score,
\citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost ensemble on 28 raw Compustat features, and
the same RUSBoost algorithm restricted to the three aggregated ARS-VG
scores as a feature-asymmetry control; (iii)~component-level analysis
decomposing Bao's aggregate AUC advantage into feature-access and
algorithm-class contributions (Section~V);
(iv)~weight-simplex sensitivity for the ensemble at a matched flag
rate (Section~IV); (v)~panel-level application of the
substitution classifier to test whether firm-level substitution is
detectable at population scale using the proposed classification
framework (Section~V); and (vi)~an ablation isolating whether
PageRank-weighted node importance specifically, as opposed to the
underlying $z$-score anomalies it weights, contributes to the graph
score's discrimination and recall (Section~V).

(1)~a vulnerability graph modeling financial-statement relationships,
(2)~graph-based anomaly detection using relative $z$-scores

with traditional Beneish and Dechow scores.

\subsection*{Vulnerability Graph Construction}
\label{subsec:graph}

A firm's financial statements are modeled as a directed graph
$\mathcal{G} = (V, E)$, where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nodes} $V = V_A \cup V_R \cup V_G$ comprise three types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Account nodes} ($V_A$): revenue, COGS, gross profit,
              operating income, net income, accounts receivable, inventory,
              accounts payable, PP\&E, and total assets.
        \item \emph{Ratio nodes} ($V_R$): DSO, DIO, DPO, and cash-conversion cycle.
        \item \emph{Governance nodes} ($V_G$): auditor type and auditor
              tenure.\footnote{$V_G$ is deliberately narrow: these are the
              only governance-related quantities wired into the graph as
              nodes with edges into the account/ratio layer. The substitution
              classifier's governance-triggered branch (Section~IV) draws on a
              separate, firm-level governance vector that additionally
              includes board independence, institutional ownership, SOX
              compliance, and analyst coverage; it is a discrete count of
              conditions rather than a single aggregated score, is computed
              outside the graph, and does not correspond one-to-one with
              $V_G$. We flag this explicitly
              rather than let the two governance representations appear
              to be the same object under different names.}
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Edges} $E$ connect nodes with known accounting relationships;
    each edge $e_{ij} \in E$ carries an expected ratio $\mu_{ij}$ and standard
    deviation $\sigma_{ij}$ estimated from cross-sectional firm-year
    distributions within each SIC group, using trimmed samples to mitigate
    the influence of outliers (Table~\ref{tab:edges}).
\end{itemize}

The graph deliberately focuses on a parsimonious set of accounting
relationships rather than enumerating all possible inter-account connections.
This design choice reflects two considerations.  First, the selected edges
correspond to the specific financial relationships that prior literature
identifies as diagnostically relevant for AEM and REM: revenue-to-receivables
\citep{beneish1999}, COGS-to-inventory \citep{roychowdhury2006}, and
income-to-cash-flow \citep{dechow2011}, ensuring that the graph structure is
\emph{theory-driven} rather than exhaustive.  Second, a parsimonious graph
preserves interpretability: each edge carries a clear economic meaning that
auditors can evaluate, whereas a dense graph would obscure the signal with
noise from uninformative relationships.  The graph-theoretic metrics
(PageRank, betweenness centrality) are intended to derive their analytical
value from the \emph{structure} of these relationships, not from sheer
graph density; an ablation reported in Section~V tests this intent
directly and finds it holds only partially (PageRank does not
outperform a naive single-node heuristic on the paper's headline
recall metric, though it does outperform uniform weighting).

For each edge, \emph{edge strain} is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Strain}(e_{ij}) = \frac{|r_{ij} - \mu_{ij}|}{\sigma_{ij}},
\label{eq:strain}
\end{equation}
where $r_{ij}$ is the observed ratio between source and target node values.
Edge strain exceeding 2.0 s.d.\ is classified as \textsc{broken}; between 1.0
and 2.0 as \textsc{stressed}; and below 1.0 as \textsc{intact}.  These
thresholds follow conventional one- and two-standard-deviation cutoffs;
we treat them as design parameters rather than empirically optimized
thresholds and assess robustness in sensitivity analyses.

\subsection*{Edge Anomaly Detection with Relative $z$-Scores}
\label{subsec:anomaly}

We apply relative $z$-scores (industry-adjusted where SIC data is
available; pooled otherwise, per the disclosure below) to five
diagnostically critical edges
that capture both AEM and REM pathways: four shared across both the
single-firm scenario pipeline and the population-scale evaluation
pipeline, and a fifth (gross margin) used only in the latter, as noted
where it is introduced below.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (cash quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{CFO}} = \frac{(\text{CFO}/\text{Revenue}) - \mu_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where superscript $(k)$ denotes the industry group (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}
    defines five such groups by SIC code); as disclosed below, the evaluation
    in Section~V uses the single pooled Default group throughout, since the
    JarFraud panel does not carry a SIC field.  A strongly
    negative $z_{\text{CFO}}$ signals revenue that does not convert to
    cash, a hallmark of premature revenue recognition (AEM).

    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Accounts Receivable} (collection quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DSO}} = \frac{\text{DSO} - \mu_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DSO} = (\text{AR}/\text{Revenue})\times 365$.  Elevated DSO
    suggests receivables growing faster than revenue, a channel-stuffing or
    fictitious-revenue signal.

    \item \textbf{COGS $\to$ Inventory} (production efficiency):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DIO}} = \frac{\text{DIO} - \mu_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DIO} = (\text{Inventory}/\text{COGS})\times 365$.  Elevated
    DIO signals overproduction to spread fixed costs, a classic REM technique
    \citep{roychowdhury2006}.

    \item \textbf{Net Income $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (accrual quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{Accrual}} = \frac{(\text{NI} - \text{CFO})/\text{TA} - \mu_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where TA denotes total assets.  A high positive $z_{\text{Accrual}}$
    indicates earnings far above cash generation, a direct AEM indicator.

    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Gross Profit} (margin quality): used only
    in the population-scale evaluation pipeline (Section~V), not in the
    four-edge single-firm scenario pipeline described above,
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{GM}} = \frac{\text{GrossMargin} - \mu_{\text{GM}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{GM}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{GrossMargin} = (\text{Revenue}-\text{COGS})/\text{Revenue}$.
    A negative $z_{\text{GM}}$ signals margin compression consistent with
    price discounting or channel stuffing, a REM indicator
    \citep{roychowdhury2006}.
\end{enumerate}

Industry benchmarks are specified for five broad SIC-based groups:
Technology (SIC 3500--3700, 7370--7379), Manufacturing (SIC 2000--3499),
Retail (SIC 5200--5999), Services (SIC 7000--8999 excluding technology),
and a Default category for all remaining sectors.
The benchmark values ($\mu$, $\sigma$) are derived from Compustat
cross-sectional medians and interquartile ranges for the respective

Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} reports the benchmark parameters.

\subsection*{Graph-Theoretic Risk Metrics}

After computing edge-level anomalies, we derive three graph-theoretic metrics
using NetworkX:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PageRank} \citep{brin1998}: identifies nodes with highest
    importance in the risk-propagation network, weighted by edge strain.
    In the population-scale evaluation pipeline (Section~V), PageRank
    enters the aggregate score directly as an importance weight on node
    strain (see GraphRisk, below); in the single-firm scenario pipeline
    (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}), it is reported for interpretability
    alongside the aggregate node risk score defined next.  An ablation
    (Section~V) shows this specific choice of importance
    weighting does not consistently outperform simpler alternatives: it
    is statistically indistinguishable from uniform node weighting on
    AUC and is significantly outperformed by a naive single-node
    heuristic on recall, the metric this paper otherwise emphasizes.
    \item \textbf{Betweenness centrality} \citep{freeman1977}: identifies
    nodes that act as critical intermediaries through which manipulation strain
    must flow.  Betweenness is computed and reported in both pipelines but
    does not currently enter either aggregate score below; we retain it
    as an interpretability diagnostic and flag its incorporation into the
    aggregate score as a natural extension (Section~VI) rather than
    implying a contribution it does not currently make.
    \item \textbf{Aggregate node risk}: a weighted combination of adjacent
    edge strain, used in the single-firm scenario pipeline
    (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}):
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Risk}(v)
        = 0.6\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\max_{e \ni v}\,\text{Strain}(e)}{3},\,1\right)
        + 0.4\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)}{2},\,1\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)$ is the mean strain of edges incident to
    node $v$.  The 0.6/0.4 weighting gives priority to peak strain over
    average strain, reflecting the forensic accounting intuition that a single
    severely broken relationship (e.g., revenue--receivables) is more
    diagnostic of manipulation than moderate strain spread across several
    edges \citep{beneish1999,dechow2011}.
\end{itemize}

The population-scale evaluation pipeline (Section~V) uses a related but
distinct \textbf{graph risk score}, built on a six-node graph (Revenue,
Accounts Receivable, Inventory, CFO, Accruals, Gross Profit) connected by
five directed edges corresponding to the accounting relationships in
Table~\ref{tab:edges} plus a revenue--gross-profit margin edge:
\begin{align}
    \text{Combined} &= 0.3\!\times\!\min(\text{NodeRisk},3)
      + 0.2\!\times\!\min(\text{PathRisk},3) \notag \\
     &\quad+ 0.25\!\times\!\min(\text{AEM}_{\text{pen}},2)
      + 0.25\!\times\!\min(\text{REM}_{\text{pen}},2), \notag \\
    \text{GraphRisk} &= \min\!\!\left(1,\;\frac{\text{Combined}}{2}\right),
    \label{eq:graphrisk}
\end{align}
where $\text{NodeRisk} = \sum_{v} \text{strain}(v)\cdot\text{PageRank}(v)$
is PageRank-weighted node strain summed across all six nodes;
$\text{PathRisk}$ is the mean edge strain across the five edges; and
$\text{AEM}_{\text{pen}}$ and $\text{REM}_{\text{pen}}$ are directional
penalty terms built on the same $z$-scores defined above, each
activating only past a 1.5-SD hinge (one standard deviation below the
2-SD \textsc{broken} cutoff already established for edge-level strain
classification) and in the manipulation-indicating direction only:
\begin{align}
    \text{AEM}_{\text{pen}} &= \max(0,\,z_{\text{Accrual}}-1.5)/2 + \max(0,\,z_{\text{DSO}}-1.5)/2, \notag \\
    \text{REM}_{\text{pen}} &= \max(0,\,-z_{\text{CFO}}-1.5)/2 + \max(0,\,z_{\text{DIO}}-1.5)/2 + \max(0,\,-z_{\text{GM}}-1.5)/2, \notag
\end{align}
with $z_{\text{GM}}$ an industry-relative gross-margin $z$-score computed
analogously to the four $z$-scores defined above (bringing the total
number of $z$-scores used in the population-scale pipeline to five,
not four; we correct this count here relative to earlier informal
description).  The $0.3$/$0.2$/$0.25$/$0.25$ weighting, like the
ensemble weights in Equation~\eqref{eq:ensemble}, is set \textit{a
priori} based on conceptual role rather than fitted to the evaluation
sample; we did not conduct a weight-simplex sensitivity analysis for
this equation specifically and identify one as a natural extension
alongside the ensemble-weight sensitivity analysis already reported in
Section~IV.  We report Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk} in this corrected
form, rather than the simpler edge-count formulation
($0.35\!\times\!n_{\textsc{broken}} + 0.15\!\times\!n_{\textsc{stressed}}
+ \overline{|z|}/4$, where $\overline{|z|}$ denotes the mean absolute
$z$-score across the five critical edges defined above for the
firm-year being scored) that motivated the design, because the corrected
form is what generated the results reported in
Table~\ref{tab:results}; the simpler formulation remains a documented
design intent that a future iteration of the artifact may converge on.

\subsection*{Traditional Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:traditional}

Two established methods are implemented for comparison and ensemble
integration.

\textbf{Beneish M-Score \citep{beneish1999}:}%
\footnote{Seven of the eight indices (DSRI, GMI, AQI, SGI, DEPI, TATA, LVGI)
are computed from JarFraud panel fields that exist in the dataset.  The
eighth, SGAI, requires SG\&A (\texttt{xsga}), which JarFraud does not carry;
the implementation's fallback branch returns exactly $1.0$ for every
firm-year rather than a genuine period-over-period SG\&A ratio.  Because
SGAI enters $M$ only as the additive term $-0.172\!\times\!\mathrm{SGAI}$
and is therefore a firm-year-invariant constant, it shifts every score by
the same amount and cannot change the rank ordering of firm-years; AUC and
the Youden-optimal-threshold precision, recall, and F1 figures reported in
Table~\ref{tab:results} are consequently unaffected by this data
limitation, though it does mean SG\&A intensity contributes no information
to the score as implemented.  Prior-year values for the other seven
indices are obtained by a panel self-join on \texttt{(gvkey, fyear$-1$)}
within JarFraud; for firm-years where a matching prior observation is not
present in the panel (approximately one-quarter of the sample, concentrated
at panel entry and after acquisitions), we use an asymmetric-proxy lag that
applies a 0.95 scaling factor to flow variables (sale, cogs, dp, lt, at,
act, rect) and the identity to stock variables that are near-invariant
period-over-period (ppegt).  This asymmetry preserves the ratio-of-ratio
signal that symmetric scaling would cancel out.  Cash flow from operations
is approximated as $\mathrm{CFO} = \mathrm{NI} + \mathrm{DP}$ because the
JarFraud panel does not carry \texttt{oancf}.  Intercept and coefficients
are taken directly from \citet{beneish1999}; no re-estimation is performed
on the evaluation sample; see the baseline re-estimation disclosure below
for a refitted comparison.}
\begin{align}
    M = \; &-4.84
      + 0.920\!\times\!\mathrm{DSRI}
      + 0.528\!\times\!\mathrm{GMI}
      + 0.404\!\times\!\mathrm{AQI}
      + 0.892\!\times\!\mathrm{SGI} \notag \\
     &+ 0.115\!\times\!\mathrm{DEPI}
      - 0.172\!\times\!\mathrm{SGAI}
      + 4.679\!\times\!\mathrm{TATA}
      - 0.327\!\times\!\mathrm{LVGI},
\end{align}
where each index is the Beneish (1999) ratio-of-ratios (DSRI: days' sales
in receivables; GMI: gross margin; AQI: asset quality; SGI: sales growth;
DEPI: depreciation; SGAI: SG\&A; TATA: total accruals over total assets;
LVGI: leverage).  The fraud probability is
$P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-(M+1.78)})$.

\textbf{Dechow F-Score \citep{dechow2011}:}%
\footnote{Five of the eight Dechow et al.\ (2011) Model~1 predictors
(\texttt{ch\_rsst}, \texttt{dch\_rec}, \texttt{dch\_inv},
\texttt{soft\_assets}, \texttt{issue}) are computed directly from
JarFraud's pre-computed lagged-differential fields, packaged into the
panel by \citet{bao2020}, so no additional Compustat merge is required to
recover them.  The remaining three predictors ($\Delta$CashSales,
$\Delta$CashMargin, $\Delta$ROA) are read from fields named
\texttt{ch\_cs}, \texttt{ch\_cm}, and \texttt{ch\_roa} in the live JarFraud
panel; our implementation instead requested \texttt{dch\_cs},
\texttt{dch\_cm}, and \texttt{dch\_roa}, which do not exist under those
names, and the lookup's fallback silently returned zero for all three
rather than raising an error.  As implemented, $F$ is therefore a
five-predictor, not an eight-predictor, score, and Table~\ref{tab:results}'s
Dechow row reflects this.  We quantified the consequence directly: applying
\citet{dechow2011}'s original coefficients to the correctly named eight
fields on the same panel and 5-fold protocol yields mean AUC $0.617\pm0.019$
(versus $0.669\pm0.015$ when the fallback zeroes the three missing
predictors, closely reproducing Table~\ref{tab:results}'s reported
$0.665\pm0.014$), so the naming defect \emph{inflates} rather than
deflates the reported Dechow AUC on this panel, and correcting only the
three field names, without re-estimating coefficients, would lower it by
roughly $0.05$ AUC.  Re-estimating all eight predictors by logistic
regression on this panel recovers AUC to $0.685\pm0.009$
(Table~\ref{tab:results}'s row is not updated with this refitted figure;
see the baseline re-estimation disclosure below for the full decomposition
and the reasoning behind reporting the as-implemented score as the
headline number). Intercept and coefficients for the headline row are
taken from the original \citet{dechow2011} Table~7 estimates; no
re-estimation is performed on the evaluation sample for that row.}
\begin{align}
    F = \; &-7.893
      + 0.790\!\times\!\mathrm{RSST}
      + 2.518\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{Rec}
      + 1.191\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{Inv}
      + 1.979\!\times\!\mathrm{SoftAssets} \notag \\
     &+ 0.171\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{CashSales}
      - 0.932\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{CashMargin}
      + 1.029\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{ROA}
      + 0.149\!\times\!\mathrm{Issue},
\end{align}
where RSST denotes \citet{richardson2005} accruals, the $\Delta$ terms are
year-over-year changes in receivables, inventories, cash sales, cash margin,
and ROA, SoftAssets is the soft-asset ratio, and $\mathrm{Issue}$ is a
binary indicator for debt or equity issuance during the fiscal year.  The
fraud probability is $P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-F})$.

\subsection*{Ensemble Method}
\label{subsec:ensemble}

Because the graph score captures relational signals while the traditional
scores capture ratio-level anomalies, we combine the three detection scores
in a weighted ensemble to exploit their complementarity:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Ensemble} = 0.25\!\times\!\hat{B}
                    + 0.35\!\times\!\hat{D}
                    + 0.40\!\times\!G_{\text{risk}},
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{B}$ is the min-max normalized Beneish probability, $\hat{D}$ is
the Dechow F-Score probability, and $G_{\text{risk}}$ is the graph risk
score from
Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk}.  The weights are set \textit{a priori} based
on conceptual considerations and are subsequently evaluated through a
comprehensive weight-simplex sensitivity analysis: the graph component receives the
highest weight (0.40) because it captures relational information absent from
the other two methods; the Dechow F-Score (0.35) is the stronger traditional
baseline in prior literature; the Beneish M-Score (0.25) provides a
complementary legacy signal.  These weights reflect a principled design
choice rather than a data-driven optimization, so their calibration consumes
no degrees of freedom from the evaluation sample.  We acknowledge, however,
that the \textit{a priori} weight specification is itself a free-parameter
choice that warrants empirical robustness testing.  We conduct a formal
sensitivity analysis over the $(\hat{B}, \hat{D}, G_{\text{risk}})$ weight simplex,
varying each weight on a 0.05 grid under the constraint that weights sum
to one (231 combinations) and re-computing fold-pooled recall at a fixed
5 percent flag rate.  Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} reports the key reference
points from this analysis; we report a summary rather than all 231 rows
here, and the full grid is reproducible in its entirety from the released
analysis code rather than gatekept behind an informal request.

statistic.

\subsection*{AEM--REM Substitution Classification}
\label{subsec:subst}

The scores $S_{\text{AEM}}$ and $S_{\text{REM}}$ used in the classification
rule below are computed by a firm-level indicator model, distinct from the
population-scale GraphRisk pipeline of Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk}: this
model powers the four-scenario demonstration in Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}
and the classification rule stated later in this subsection, and requires
inputs (R\&D expense, SG\&A expense, advertising expense) that can be
supplied for the hand-specified scenario firms but are not reliably
available across the JarFraud panel, which is why it is not the scoring
mechanism used for the population-scale evaluation in Section~V.

$S_{\text{AEM}}$ aggregates three indicators.  The first is a
stylized discretionary-accrual measure in the spirit of the modified
Jones model \citep{dechow1995}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{DA} = \frac{\text{NI}-\text{CFO}}{\text{TA}}
    - \left(0.01 + 0.03\,\frac{\Delta\text{Rev}-\Delta\text{AR}}{\text{TA}}
    - 0.05\,\frac{\text{PPE}}{\text{TA}}\right),
    \label{eq:styljones}
\end{equation}
standardized by a fixed benchmark standard deviation ($0.05$) rather than
an industry-year first-stage regression.\footnote{This is a material
simplification relative to the modified Jones model as used in the
archival earnings-management literature, where the nondiscretionary
accrual coefficients are estimated industry-year by industry-year rather
than fixed at $0.01/0.03/0.05$ as here.  We use the fixed-coefficient
version because it requires no auxiliary industry-year estimation sample
and can be evaluated on a single hand-specified firm-year, which the
scenario demonstration in Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} requires; it should
not be read as a claim of equivalence to a properly estimated
discretionary-accrual residual.  A fully estimated version is a natural
extension for any future panel-scale use of this indicator model.} The
second and third are the period-over-period change in days-sales-outstanding
and in gross margin, each standardized by a fixed benchmark standard
deviation ($5$ days and $0.02$, respectively); both require a prior-year
observation and are therefore only computed where one is available.
$S_{\text{REM}}$ aggregates up to four indicators built on the
\citet{roychowdhury2006} REM framework in similarly stylized
form: abnormal CFO, abnormal production costs, abnormal discretionary
expenses (R\&D, SG\&A, and advertising relative to sales), and the
period-over-period change in days-inventory-outstanding; each computed
as an actual-versus-expected deviation against a fixed linear benchmark
in revenue and standardized by a fixed benchmark standard deviation, in
the same spirit as Equation~\eqref{eq:styljones}.\footnote{As with the
AEM indicators, the ``expected'' benchmarks here are fixed linear
functions of revenue rather than coefficients estimated industry-year by
industry-year as in \citet{roychowdhury2006}; the same caveat applies.}
Each indicator's standardized deviation is retained only in the
manipulation-indicating direction (e.g., abnormally \emph{low} CFO
counts toward $S_{\text{REM}}$, abnormally high CFO does not) and only
past a zero floor, then averaged and divided by $3$ before capping at
$1$:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{AEM}} = \min\!\left(\frac{1}{3n_{\text{AEM}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{AEM}}}\max(0,\pm z_i),\,1\right),
\end{equation}
and analogously for $S_{\text{REM}}$, where $n_{\text{AEM}} \in \{1,2,3\}$
and $n_{\text{REM}} \in \{1,\dots,4\}$ depend on whether a prior-year
observation is available and, for $S_{\text{REM}}$, whether discretionary-expense
inputs are supplied.

Substitution classification proceeds through an ordered decision
sequence, evaluated until one branch applies, using
$\theta_{\text{AEM}} = 0.30$ and $\theta_{\text{REM}} = 0.40$ throughout
(calibrated at the 70th percentile of the respective score
distributions across training folds):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item If $S_{\text{AEM}} \geq \theta_{\text{AEM}}$ and $S_{\text{REM}} < \theta_{\text{REM}}$: \textsc{aem-dominant}.
    \item Else if $S_{\text{REM}} \geq \theta_{\text{REM}}$ and $S_{\text{AEM}} < \theta_{\text{AEM}}$: \textsc{rem-dominant}.
    \item Else if $S_{\text{AEM}} \geq \theta_{\text{AEM}}$ and $S_{\text{REM}} \geq \theta_{\text{REM}}$: \textsc{parallel} (both channels active; no governance condition is evaluated at this step).
    \item Else if $S_{\text{AEM}} > 0.3$ and $S_{\text{REM}} > 0.3$: a severity-based route distinguishes REM$\to$AEM from AEM$\to$REM by comparing high-severity indicator counts.  Section~V (Panel-Level Substitution Analysis) shows this route is arithmetically unreachable once $\theta_{\text{AEM}} = 0.30$ exactly, since it requires $S_{\text{AEM}}$ simultaneously below and above the same value.
    \item Else if governance information is supplied and $S_{\text{REM}} \geq \theta_{\text{REM}}/2$: count how many of three conditions hold (Big~4 auditor; SOX compliance jointly with institutional ownership exceeding 50 percent; board independence exceeding 60 percent) and flag AEM$\to$REM if at least two hold.  This governance-triggered branch operationalizes the prediction that strong governance \emph{suppresses} AEM below its normal detection level while redirecting manipulation pressure to REM, the balloon-effect pattern described by \citeauthor{zang2012} \citeyearpar{zang2012}, as a discrete count of conditions rather than a single continuous governance score.
    \item Otherwise: no pattern detected.
\end{enumerate}
\label{eq:classrule}

We state this as an explicit sequence, rather than a single compact
condition, because the branches are ordered alternatives: a firm-year
satisfying an earlier branch never reaches a later one, and only the
fifth branch depends on governance at all.  Governance information is
tracked as a five-field vector (auditor type, institutional ownership,
board independence, SOX compliance, and analyst coverage); only the
first three of these enter the decision rule, through the named
conditions above, and analyst coverage is tracked but does not
currently enter any branch, the same reporting-without-use status as
betweenness centrality in Section~IV.  The implementation that
produced Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} and the Section~V panel-level
results does not compute a single aggregated governance score compared
against its own sample median; we correct the description here to
match the discrete rule that was actually run, consistent with the
disclosure already applied to Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk}.

\textbf{A note on discrete governance gating under covariate sparsity.}
The rule above requires several governance conditions to jointly hold
before the branch activates.  Section~V reports that, when applied at
panel scale under a specification in which most governance inputs must
be held at fixed values (Section~V, Panel-Level Substitution Analysis),
this discrete gating structure does not activate for any firm-year in
the JarFraud panel, a mathematical property of the rule under those
conditions, not a data-driven null finding.  We discuss the general
mechanism, and its implications for discrete threshold-gated governance
classifiers more broadly, in Section~V and the Limitations
(Section~VI).

\subsection*{Dataset}

We use the JarFraud dataset \citep{bao2020}, comprising 146{,}045 firm-year
observations for U.S.\ SEC registrants spanning 1990--2014.  Binary fraud
labels are derived from SEC Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Releases
(AAERs), with AAER coverage most complete through 2008; post-2008
observations carry substantially fewer confirmed labels due to enforcement
lag.  Of the full panel, 964 firm-years (0.7 percent) carry confirmed fraud
labels.  Financial variables are sourced from Compustat via the JarFraud
panel.  No industry exclusions are applied; all SIC sectors are retained to
preserve population representativeness.

An important conceptual note: the theoretical framework of this paper
concerns \emph{earnings management} (both legal and aggressive), while the
JarFraud labels identify \emph{fraud} (material misstatements subject to SEC
enforcement).  AAER-labeled fraud represents the most extreme end of the
earnings management spectrum, implying that the evaluation focuses on
detecting extreme manifestations of manipulation rather than the full
spectrum of earnings management behavior.  We use AAER fraud labels as an empirical proxy
because they are the only large-scale, externally validated binary labels
available for earnings manipulation research.  The graph-based anomaly
detection is designed to capture the \emph{relational strain} that
characterizes manipulation broadly, of which enforcement-level fraud is the
observable subset.  This construct gap has two implications for
interpretation.  First, the low precision (0.8 percent) overstates the
``false-positive'' rate: some flagged firms may engage in aggressive but
non-fraudulent earnings management that produces relational strain
detectable by the graph yet falls below the SEC enforcement threshold.
Second, recall is measured only against the observable fraud subset;
the artifact's sensitivity to milder manipulation cannot be assessed
with AAER labels alone and remains a direction for future research using
discretionary-accrual proxies or restatement databases.

\textbf{Robustness to CV protocol and to the Bao comparison's observation
count, and disclosure of the Bao et al.\ (2020) erratum.} Three
protocol choices in Table~\ref{tab:results} depart from
\citet{bao2020}'s own evaluation design and warrant direct disclosure
rather than a citation alone.  First, the 5-fold split used throughout is
stratified by fraud label but not grouped by firm; because AAER fraud
spells can span multiple consecutive firm-years, a firm's fraud years
could in principle be split across training and test folds within the
same split.  We re-ran the full Beneish/Dechow/Graph/Ensemble comparison
using \texttt{GroupKFold} on firm identifier (\texttt{gvkey}) instead of
plain stratified k-fold; the resulting AUCs (Beneish $0.570$, Dechow
$0.667$, Graph $0.551$, Ensemble $0.553$) are within $0.002$ of
Table~\ref{tab:results}'s reported values, indicating the firm-grouping
concern, while theoretically real, does not materially affect the
reported comparison.  Second, \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost benchmark and
the ARS-VG methods could in principle be evaluated on different subsets
of the panel if rows with missing ratio inputs were excluded
asymmetrically; we confirmed by direct comparison that both arms of
Table~\ref{tab:results} are evaluated on the identical $N=146{,}045$
observations, so the head-to-head comparison is not confounded by an
observation-count mismatch.  Third, \citet{walker2021a,walker2021b}
identified an error in \citet{bao2020}'s original recoding of
spanning serial fraud (fraud spells that cross the training/test
boundary in a time-forward evaluation), corrected in
\citet{bao2022erratum}.  That correction is specific to a
time-forward train/test design and to the particular recoding
procedure \citet{bao2020} applied to it; our evaluation uses random
stratified $k$-fold CV rather than a time-forward split and does not
implement any version of that recoding procedure, buggy or corrected.
We disclose this rather than claim compliance with either version of
Bao's procedure: our design substitutes a different (and, per the
\texttt{GroupKFold} check above, empirically similar) exposure to
firm-level correlation for the specific exposure the erratum
addresses, and readers should not infer that Table~\ref{tab:results}'s
Bao et al.\ (2020) row reproduces \citeauthor{bao2020}'s own reported
evaluation protocol.

\textbf{Baseline re-estimation: refitting the parametric benchmarks on
this panel.} Reviewers of an earlier version of this manuscript correctly
noted that Table~\ref{tab:results}'s Beneish and Dechow rows apply
coefficients estimated on samples that predate JarFraud's 1990--2014
evaluation window without any re-estimation, and asked whether refitting
on the evaluation panel changes the comparison.  We tested this directly:
using the same 8 raw ratio inputs each fixed-coefficient formula already
consumes, the same 5 folds, and the same protocol as Table~\ref{tab:results}
(fit on train, score on test, no leakage), we replaced each paper's fixed
weights with a logistic regression (\texttt{StandardScaler}-normalized
features, \texttt{class\_weight=`balanced'}) fit independently within each
training fold.  Refitting raises Beneish's mean AUC from
$0.571\pm0.024$ to $0.616\pm0.020$ ($+0.045$) and Dechow's from
$0.665\pm0.014$ to $0.685\pm0.009$ ($+0.020$); recall at the
refitted Youden point is essentially unchanged for both (Beneish
$60.6\%\rightarrow59.5\%$; Dechow $67.4\%\rightarrow66.5\%$).  For
Dechow, the footnote above decomposes this $+0.020$ further into a
column-naming bug-fix effect ($-0.052$ AUC, i.e., correcting the defect
alone, without refitting, makes the original-coefficient score \emph{worse}
on this panel) and a re-estimation effect ($+0.068$ AUC) that more than
offsets it; the refitted model's own coefficients (mean across folds:
$\Delta$CashSales $+0.12$, $\Delta$CashMargin $-0.02$, $\Delta$ROA $-0.11$,
versus the original paper's $+0.171$, $-0.932$, $+1.029$) differ enough in
sign and relative magnitude from \citet{dechow2011}'s published estimates
to indicate genuine coefficient staleness rather than measurement noise.
For Beneish, the refitted SGAI coefficient is estimated at $0.0000$ across
every fold, consistent with the footnote above: SGAI is a constant on this
panel because the underlying \texttt{xsga} field is unavailable, so no
finite amount of refitting can recover information from it.  We read this
exercise as supporting, rather than overturning, Limitation~Sixth below:
neither traditional baseline is competitive with modern
supervised methods (Table~\ref{tab:results}'s Bao et al.\ RUSBoost row,
AUC $0.812$) whether evaluated with fixed 1999/2011 coefficients or
refitted on this panel, so refitting narrows but does not close the gap
motivating ARS-VG's graph-based approach.  We report
Table~\ref{tab:results}'s Beneish and Dechow rows using the original,
unrefitted coefficients throughout the rest of this paper, consistent
with our framing of these methods as literature benchmarks applied
as-published rather than as models tuned to this evaluation panel, and
present the refitted figures here as a supplementary robustness check
rather than substituting them into Table~\ref{tab:results}, so that no
downstream statistic (McNemar tests, feature-asymmetry analysis, temporal
validation) needs to be re-derived against a moving baseline definition.

\subsubsection*{Operating-point profile}
\label{subsec:operating-point}

Aggregate AUC comparisons obscure a first-order distinction in where the
methods add value.  At strict operating points the ordering is Bao
$>$ Dechow $>$ Ensemble: RUSBoost's recall at a 5 percent flag rate is
28.7 percent, while Dechow achieves 11.7 percent and the ARS-VG Ensemble 4.0 percent.
At the Youden-optimal threshold, which is permissive enough to flag on
the order of half the panel, the ordering reverses for recall: Ensemble
77.1 percent $>$ Dechow 67.4 percent $>$ RUSBoost 57.1 percent.  The artifact therefore
occupies a distinct region of the operating curve, the high-recall,
high-flag end, that is structurally different from the discrimination
frontier explored by gradient-boosted tree ensembles.  Which operating
point is appropriate depends on downstream use: an audit team with finite
triage capacity will prefer Bao; a researcher or regulator screening
for \emph{substitution-consistent} firms at population scale will find
the Ensemble's operating profile more useful.

\subsubsection*{Feature asymmetry: decomposing Bao's advantage}
\label{subsec:feature-asymmetry}

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:results} is not a like-for-like contrast
of algorithmic sophistication.  ARS-VG's Ensemble is a linear combination
of three aggregated, theoretically interpretable scalars
$\{\hat{B}, \hat{D}, G_{\text{risk}}\}$; \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost is a gradient-boosted
ensemble over 28 raw Compustat variables.  The two methods differ in
algorithm class \emph{and} in feature access.  To isolate which factor
drives the AUC gap, we retrain RUSBoost using the same hyperparameters
and the same cross-validation folds, but restricted to the three
aggregated scores.  The result (Table~\ref{tab:results}, second-to-last
row) is AUC $=0.618$: a $+0.065$ gain over the linear Ensemble
($0.553$), and a $-0.194$ shortfall relative to the 28-feature
configuration ($0.812$).  Roughly three-quarters of Bao's aggregate
advantage on JarFraud is therefore attributable to richer feature
access, not to non-linear learning per se.

This decomposition has both a defensive and a constructive implication.
Defensively, it contextualizes the AUC gap: ARS-VG is not a weaker
detector because graph-theoretic methods are inherently less powerful
than gradient boosting; it is a less discriminating ranker on JarFraud
primarily because its inputs are three interpretable aggregates rather
than twenty-eight raw ledger items.  Constructively, it prices the
specific interpretability choice this artifact embodies: moving from
\citet{bao2020}'s 28-feature Compustat specification to ARS-VG's three
channel-level aggregates costs $0.194$ AUC on JarFraud under an
identical RUSBoost pipeline.  We do not claim this is a per-feature
constant that generalizes to other feature sets or other panels: the
quantity depends on which 28 features are available and which
three aggregates are chosen, and a linear extrapolation is not
supported by the two-point measurement we report.  We claim only that
on this specific benchmark the feature-aggregation cost is a definable
quantity that practitioners can weigh against the interpretability
and channel-decomposition properties the aggregation enables, rather
than a latent confound in method comparisons.

\subsubsection*{Ablation: does PageRank-weighted node importance earn its keep?}
\label{subsec:pagerank-ablation}

Section~IV notes that PageRank enters $\text{NodeRisk}$ as an

choice, rather than the underlying $z$-score anomalies it weights, is
doing useful work, we recomputed GraphRisk under three node-importance
schemes: PageRank (as reported), uniform weighting across all six
nodes, and a naive scheme that places all weight on whichever single
node carries the highest strain, holding PathRisk, the AEM/REM
penalty terms, the ensemble weights, and the CV protocol (identical
folds, paired across all three arms) exactly fixed.

graph score's recall advantage over the parametric baselines: a naive
single-node heuristic recovers more recall than PageRank does on this
benchmark, even though PageRank remains preferable to uniformly
averaging strain across all nodes.  Precision and F1 differences are
statistically significant in the same paired sense but are two to three
orders of magnitude smaller in absolute terms (on the order of
$0.03$--$0.12$ percentage points), consistent with this paper's
broader point that precision is uniformly poor across every scheme at
this operating point and is not the axis on which these schemes
usefully differ.

\textbf{Reading the AUC honestly.}  An AUC of $0.550$ indicates that the
graph risk score has very little ranking power across the full score
distribution.  Part of this is structural: the graph risk score
(Equation~\ref{eq:graphrisk}) is a bounded, non-probabilistic aggregate
that compresses discrimination across the score range, whereas the Dechow
F-Score is a smooth parametric probability.  But we do not attempt to
argue that the bounded construction \emph{explains away} the low AUC.
The graph layer is informative at the Youden operating point and not
much beyond it; it is not a standalone ranker, and it should not be
interpreted as one.  The ensemble's recall gain of 9.7 percentage points
over Dechow (77.1 percent vs.\ 67.4 percent) represents fraud firm-years that the
Dechow F-Score alone fails to flag: cases where manipulation creates
relational strain across connected accounts rather than isolated ratio
anomalies.  This complementarity, rather than ranking superiority,
is what motivates the ensemble.

\textbf{Reading the precision honestly.}  At a 0.7 percent base rate and
$N = 146{,}045$ firm-years, a Youden-optimal threshold that achieves
75--77 percent recall necessarily flags on the order of ninety thousand
firm-years to capture roughly seven hundred true fraud cases.  This is
not an audit-ready screen.  No audit team can follow up on ninety thousand
flags, and the \citet{karpoff2008} asymmetric-cost argument (that firms
lose \$3.08 in market value for every \$1 of fraudulent inflation revealed,
which superficially favors high-recall screening) does not by itself
rescue an operating point at this density of alerts.  We therefore
position ARS-VG, in its current form, as a \emph{research prototype} whose
practical deployment requires one of three forms of further calibration
beyond the scope of this paper: (i) operating at a substantially stricter
threshold and reporting the full precision--recall curve rather than a
single Youden point; (ii) embedding the graph score in a multi-stage
triage ensemble in which downstream stages raise precision; or
(iii) reserving the graph score for the relational substitution-classification
function (Section~IV) for which it is uniquely
suited and using parametric scores for absolute risk ranking.  A full
precision--recall curve over stricter thresholds is available from the
authors on reasonable request; the head-to-head comparison
against \citet{bao2020} is reported in Table~\ref{tab:results} and
Section~V.

Temporal validation (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}) shows that all four
methods generalize to the held-out 2006--2014 period with less than
10 percent AUC change, confirming robustness beyond the training window.

it is not independent evidence that the classifier detects
substitution behavior in data whose labels were not derived from the
same theoretical priors used to write the rule.  Genuine construct
validation would require the classifier to recover
substitution-consistent patterns in data external to its own design
assumptions: precisely the test that Section~V's panel-level
evaluation attempts and reports as unsuccessful.  We therefore treat
the scenario tests as rule-execution verification rather than
construct validation, and revise the contribution claim accordingly
(Section~VI): ARS-VG demonstrates that the AEM--REM substitution
hypothesis can be encoded into a graph-based classifier that executes
correctly on illustrative cases; it does not yet show that firm-level
substitution is independently observable.  The substitution case
remains useful as an illustration of the intended mechanism: a
single-channel detector would miss SubstitutionPattern Inc.\ entirely,
whereas ARS-VG's governance-aware branch correctly implements the
balloon-effect logic it was designed to implement.

\subsection*{Panel-Level Substitution Analysis: A Negative Result and Its Mechanism}
\label{subsec:panel-substitution}

To test TC1 empirically (that firm-level AEM$\to$REM substitution is
\emph{observable} in realized data and not merely a demonstrable
architectural feature), we applied the substitution classifier to all
146{,}045 firm-years in the JarFraud panel.  Because JarFraud does not
carry the auditor, institutional-ownership, or board-independence
variables required for the full governance-moderated branch, we held
those three inputs at neutral median defaults (Non-Big4, 50 percent
institutional ownership, 0.5 board independence) and activated SOX
compliance as the only time-varying governance lever, true for fiscal
years $\geq 2003$ and false otherwise, the same design used for the
scenario tests in Section~V.

This test does not succeed, and we report why rather than reporting a
number.  The classifier's decision rule (Section~IV) contains two
routes by which a firm-year can be flagged as AEM$\to$REM substitution:
a severity-based route requiring $S_{\text{AEM}} > 0.3$ and
$S_{\text{REM}} > 0.3$ while having already failed the primary
threshold checks at $\theta_{\text{AEM}} = 0.30$, and a
governance-triggered route requiring at least two of three governance
conditions (Big~4 auditor; SOX compliance jointly with institutional
ownership exceeding 50 percent; board independence exceeding 60
percent).  Under the panel's neutral-default specification, both routes
are unreachable as a matter of arithmetic, not data.  The severity route
requires $S_{\text{AEM}}$ to be simultaneously below $\theta_{\text{AEM}}
= 0.30$ (to have failed the primary check) and above $0.3$ (to satisfy
the severity check), the same numerical value under two strict
inequalities of opposite direction, which no value of $S_{\text{AEM}}$
can satisfy.  The governance route requires two of three conditions;
under the stated neutral defaults, auditor type never satisfies
``Big~4,'' board independence of $0.5$ never exceeds $0.6$, and
institutional ownership held at exactly $50$ never satisfies the strict
inequality ``exceeds $50$'' even after SOX compliance turns on in 2003.
No governance condition is ever satisfied, so the count is always zero.
The substitution flag is therefore identically zero for every firm-year
in the panel under this specification, a property of the classifier's
decision rule interacting with the panel's covariate availability, which
we verified by exhaustive enumeration over the relevant score range in
addition to deriving it analytically.

We regard this as a genuine, reportable finding rather than an
implementation failure to be silently corrected.  A discrete,
threshold-gated governance rule that requires a fixed count of
conditions to hold is only informative when enough of its inputs
actually vary; when data availability forces most inputs to a constant
(as JarFraud's lack of auditor, ownership, and board-independence
history forces here), a $k$-of-$n$ counting rule with $k \geq 2$ can
be rendered permanently inert by the arithmetic of the rule itself, in
a way that is easy to miss precisely because the rule appears to test
a rich multi-factor interaction.  We flag this as a caution for
discrete governance-gated classifiers more generally, not only for
ARS-VG: a classifier of this form should be checked for reachability
under its anticipated deployment covariates before its absence of
positive flags is interpreted as a substantive null result.

Two consequences follow for the remainder of this paper.  First, we do
not report a population-scale test of the SOX-driven ``balloon effect''
(TC3): the architectural claim, that the classifier's governance-aware
branch correctly identifies the balloon-effect pattern when governance
inputs vary, as demonstrated in the SubstitutionPattern Inc.\ scenario
of Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}, stands on its own, but we withdraw any
claim that this pattern was validated at panel scale.  Second, the
construct-validation exercise we had planned against the Richardson RSST
accrual measure is not meaningful here: correlating a variable that is
identically zero against any other variable is not a validity test, so
we do not report it.  A genuine panel-scale test of TC1/TC3 would require a
Compustat/BoardEx merge, noted elsewhere in this paper
(Section~VI) as a natural extension; that merge would give board
independence, institutional ownership, and auditor type as genuinely
time-varying, firm-level covariates, which is the condition under which
this classifier's governance-gated branch is designed to be informative.
We describe this extension because it follows directly from the
mechanism identified above, not because it is a planned or funded
activity at the time of writing.

\subsection*{Theoretical Contributions}

\textbf{TC1: Firm-level substitution framework (demonstrated, not
population-validated).}  ARS-VG is, to our
knowledge, the first computational artifact to operationalize the AEM--REM
substitution hypothesis as a firm-level scoring and classification system.
By jointly modeling both channels in a shared graph, the artifact makes
substitution \emph{observable} at the individual-firm level in the four
theoretically calibrated scenarios of Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}, including
a governance-moderated case that a single-channel detector would miss
entirely.  We had intended this firm-level observability to extend to
direct empirical examination at population scale, addressing the
methodological concerns raised by \citet{cohenlys2022} about whether
aggregate substitution measures capture firm-level strategic behavior;
Section~V shows this population-scale extension is not achieved
under the JarFraud panel's governance-covariate constraints, for reasons
we characterize precisely there.  We therefore claim TC1 as a
demonstrated architectural capability, not as a population-validated
measurement instrument.

\textbf{TC2: Relational view of earnings management detection.}  The
application of graph-theoretic strain propagation to financial-statement
structures extends the earnings management detection literature beyond the
ratio-decomposition paradigm.  The graph perspective reveals that
manipulation creates multi-account relational strain, a pattern invisible
to methods that treat financial variables as independent, aligning with
\citet{march1995}'s observation that IS artifacts often produce theoretical
insights unavailable through conventional analytical methods.

\textbf{TC3: Governance-moderated substitution, architecturally
integrated, empirically unreachable under the tested specification.}
The substitution classifier integrates
four governance constraints (auditor type, institutional ownership,
board independence, and SOX compliance) into the
substitution-classification stage, operationalizing the
agency-theoretic prediction \citep{jensen1976} that strong governance
suppresses AEM while redirecting manipulation to REM.  Of the four
constraints, only SOX compliance is derivable from fiscal-year data
available in JarFraud; the other three require firm-year governance
panels that JarFraud does not carry.  We therefore assess TC3 in two
forms.  Architecturally, all four constraints are exercised correctly by the
four scenario tests in Section~V (a rule-execution check, not an
independent construct-validity test; see Section~V), which confirm correct
regime classification under each governance configuration, including the
governance-triggered balloon-effect case.  Empirically, applying the
SOX arm to all 146{,}045 JarFraud firm-years (Section~V) shows the
classifier's decision rule to be arithmetically unreachable under the
panel's neutral-default specification for the remaining three
constraints: not a weak or partial signal, but an identically-zero
flag rate that follows from the rule's own structure.  A full
panel-scale test of TC3 would require a Compustat/BoardEx merge that is
outside the scope of this paper; we describe this as a natural
extension that follows from the mechanism identified above, not as a
planned activity.  We therefore claim TC3 as an architectural
contribution supported by scenario-level demonstration, together with
a precise, verified account of why population-scale testing requires
richer governance data than the public benchmark provides, not as a
validated governance-regime test at panel scale.

\textbf{TC4: Quantified feature-aggregation cost of interpretability.}
A recurring concern in accounting-AI research is that the tradeoff
between black-box predictive accuracy and interpretable aggregated
signals is discussed qualitatively but rarely priced.  The
feature-asymmetry decomposition reported in Section~V gives a concrete
price for this tradeoff on a standard benchmark: holding algorithm class
constant, aggregating from twenty-eight raw Compustat features to three
channel-level scalars costs $0.194$ AUC on JarFraud.  Holding feature set
constant, moving from a linear ensemble to RUSBoost buys $0.065$ AUC.
This decomposition provides a priceable quantity that practitioners
and researchers can weigh against the interpretability and
channel-decomposition properties the aggregation enables, rather than
leaving the tradeoff as a latent confound in method comparisons.

We present two readings of this measurement rather than only the one
favorable to the artifact.  Read constructively, it prices a specific,
deliberate design choice: practitioners who need channel-level
decomposition can weigh a known $0.194$-AUC cost against that
requirement rather than treating the cost as an unmeasured confound.
Read skeptically, the identical result is equally consistent with the
conclusion that ARS-VG's aggregation from twenty-eight raw features to
three channel-level scalars discards real predictive information
without this paper demonstrating a compensating benefit beyond
interpretability itself: a cost whose offsetting return is asserted,
not shown, by our evaluation.  We do not adjudicate between these
readings analytically; which one is more apt depends on how much a
given application values channel-level decomposition relative to raw
discrimination, a judgment this paper deliberately leaves to the
reader rather than resolving in the artifact's favor.  Of
the four theoretical contributions claimed in this paper, TC4 rests on
the one measurement that is fully verified and reproduced, independent
of the governance-covariate and industry-adjustment limitations
discussed above and in Section~VI (Limitations); whether that
measurement counts as evidence for or against the artifact's design is,
per the preceding discussion, a matter of interpretation rather than a
settled empirical fact.

\subsection*{Practical Contributions}

\textbf{PC1: Research infrastructure for population-scale
substitution analysis (contingent on richer governance data).}  The
practical contribution of ARS-VG is \emph{not} audit-team deployment.
At the Youden operating point the ensemble achieves the highest recall
of any tested method, but it does so by flagging a share of the panel
that no audit team can triage; at strict low-flag-rate operating points
\citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost dominates on precision and recall alike and
is the appropriate instrument for auditor-facing use.  We had intended
the substitution flag itself to serve as a firm-year dependent variable
for panel studies of earnings management and governance--substitution
interactions of the kind envisaged in TC3; Section~V shows this flag is
identically zero under JarFraud's covariate-sparse governance
specification, so we do not claim this capability as delivered.  A
Compustat/BoardEx merge is what would be needed to realize this
capability: once
auditor type, institutional ownership, and board independence are
available as genuinely time-varying, firm-level covariates, the
governance-gated branch is designed to produce a non-degenerate flag
suitable for this purpose.  We note this as the technical condition
under which PC1 would be delivered, not as a commitment to undertake
that merge.  Deployment as a fraud-detection screen was
never the use case we support, independent of this limitation.

\textbf{PC2: Regulatory monitoring.}  The SEC EDGAR integration enables
scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant population without
requiring proprietary data access.

\textbf{Realistic near-term applications.}  Given the evaluation
boundary characterized above, we identify three uses consistent with
what this paper actually demonstrates, rather than a broader
audit-deployment claim.  First, the feature-asymmetry decomposition
(TC4) is directly usable by practitioners deciding between an
interpretable, channel-decomposed model and a higher-discrimination
black-box model: $0.194$ AUC is a concrete number a firm or regulator
can weigh against the audit-trail and explainability requirements of
a given deployment context, independent of whether ARS-VG specifically
is adopted.  Second, the SEC EDGAR pipeline (PC2) is usable today for
CIK-level screening of individual registrants using structured filing
data that does carry industry (SIC) information, distinct from the
JarFraud-based population evaluation in Section~V.  Third, the
four-scenario demonstration (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}) is usable as a
training or illustrative tool for auditors and analysts learning to
recognize the qualitative signature of AEM-dominant, REM-dominant, and
governance-moderated substitution patterns in financial statements,
independent of the panel-scale validation question this paper leaves
open.  We do not claim a fourth use, automated population-scale
fraud or substitution screening, because Section~V's evaluation does
not support it.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Seven limitations bound the current findings and warrant explicit
discussion.

\emph{First, ARS-VG does not dominate modern supervised machine-learning
fraud detectors on aggregate ranking.}  We report the head-to-head
comparison against \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost ensemble on the same
5-fold splits in Table~\ref{tab:results}: Bao achieves AUC $0.812$
versus the ARS-VG Ensemble's $0.553$, a $+0.259$ gap.  The
feature-asymmetry decomposition in Section~V
attributes approximately $+0.194$ of this gap to Bao's access to
twenty-eight raw Compustat features and only $+0.065$ to non-linear
learning; the remaining gap can reasonably be attributed to the
interpretability-driven choice to aggregate channel-level signals into
three scalars.  ARS-VG is therefore not a replacement for gradient-boosted
ML on absolute-risk ranking; it is a Design Science Research
artifact whose contribution is to make firm-level AEM--REM substitution
\emph{observable} while retaining channel-level interpretability.
Still unresolved are (i) whether the relational signal captured by the
graph is \emph{additive} to the signal captured by gradient-boosted
ensembles in a stacked configuration, (ii) whether adding transformer-based
text encoders over MD\&A filings \citep{craja2020} closes further
gaps, and (iii) whether a graph neural network operating on the same
28-feature panel would preserve interpretability without the
$0.194$-AUC aggregation cost.  These are the priority extensions.

\emph{Second, the panel-level substitution analysis (TC1 empirical
test) does not succeed, for a reason we can state precisely rather
than approximately.}  Results in Section~V show that under JarFraud's
neutral-default governance specification, both routes in the
substitution classifier's decision rule are unreachable as a matter of
arithmetic: the severity-based route requires a score to be
simultaneously below and above the same threshold value, and the
governance-triggered route requires two of three conditions that can
never jointly hold when auditor type, institutional ownership, and
board independence are fixed constants.  The substitution flag is
therefore identically zero for the entire panel, which forecloses any
correlational test against it, including the Richardson RSST
construct-validation exercise we had planned: correlating a constant
against anything is not informative, so we do not report it.  We
regard the precise, verified mechanism behind this null result (rather
than the null result alone) as the more useful finding: it identifies
a general failure mode for discrete, $k$-of-$n$ threshold-gated
governance classifiers under covariate-sparse deployment conditions,
independent of this specific artifact.  A full test of TC3, the
governance-moderated branch, would require merging JarFraud onto Compustat
and BoardEx to obtain firm-level auditor, institutional-ownership, and
board-independence variables as genuinely time-varying covariates.
We describe this merge because the mechanism identified above shows
precisely why it would be needed, not because it is a planned or
funded activity; no population-scale claim about governance-moderated
substitution can be made without it, or without some other source of
genuinely time-varying governance data.

\emph{Third, precision at the reported operating point is not
audit-ready.}  As discussed in Section~V, at a 0.7 percent base
rate a 75--77 percent recall implies flagging on the order of ninety thousand
firm-years to capture roughly seven hundred true positives.  The paper
therefore positions ARS-VG as a research prototype, not a deployable
screen, and identifies full PR-curve reporting and stricter operating
points as a required next step.

\emph{Fourth, AAER label scope and coverage.}  The JarFraud AAER labels
are most complete through 2008; performance on post-2008 reporting
environments, including IFRS-influenced periods, has not been tested
with comprehensive labels, and \citet{pincus2022}'s evidence that
substitution dynamics are time-varying warrants caution in generalizing
to more recent periods.  Additionally, AAER captures only fraud severe
enough to trigger SEC enforcement; aggressive-but-legal earnings
management is not separately labeled, which biases the measured
precision downward.

\emph{Fifth, ensemble weights are set \textit{a priori} rather than
learned.}  The weights in Equation~\eqref{eq:ensemble} are chosen on
the basis of conceptual role, not fitted on the evaluation sample.
The weight-simplex sensitivity analysis reported in
Section~IV quantifies the consequence: at a matched
5 percent flag rate, recall varies from 4.0 percent to 12.4 percent across the
simplex, and only 1.3 percent of weight configurations meet or exceed the
Dechow-only recall benchmark (11.7 percent).  The weight choice is
therefore load-bearing in the strict-operating-point regime, though
it does not affect the qualitative ranking at the Youden point.
Data-driven weight selection via Bayesian optimization, stacking, or
isotonic calibration over cross-validation folds is a natural
extension.

\emph{Sixth, the parametric benchmarks combine dated, unrefitted
coefficients with a period-mismatched evaluation panel, and we have
tested rather than merely disclosed this.}  The Beneish
M-Score coefficients are estimated on a pre-1999 sample and the Dechow
F-Score coefficients on a pre-2011 sample (see the footnotes to
Section~IV); both are applied to the 1990--2014 JarFraud panel without
re-estimation in Table~\ref{tab:results}, and the ensemble weights
combining them with the graph score are themselves set \textit{a
priori} rather than fitted (Section~IV).  We directly quantified the
consequence (Section~V, baseline re-estimation disclosure): refitting
both baselines via logistic regression on this panel's own training
folds raises Beneish's AUC by $0.045$ (to $0.616$) and Dechow's by
$0.020$ (to $0.685$), so vintage mismatch is a real, measurable drag on
both benchmarks rather than a hypothetical concern, but it is not large
enough for either refitted baseline to approach the modern
supervised-learning benchmark (Bao et al.'s RUSBoost, AUC $0.812$).
This confirms that neither parametric benchmark, whether run with its
original coefficients or re-estimated on this panel, is competitive
with feature-rich supervised learning, which is the comparison ARS-VG's
graph-based approach is positioned against; it does not, by itself,
validate ARS-VG's own AUC, which remains below both the fixed and the
refitted parametric baselines (Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\emph{Seventh, PageRank-weighted node importance is not the source of
the graph score's recall advantage, and its value is metric-dependent.}
The ablation reported in Section~V (Table~\ref{tab:pagerank-ablation})
tests whether PageRank weighting specifically, as opposed to the
underlying $z$-score anomalies it weights, is doing useful work,
holding every other component of GraphRisk and the CV protocol fixed.
The two metrics this paper reports disagree: PageRank is statistically
indistinguishable from uniform node weighting on AUC and significantly
better than a naive single-node heuristic on AUC, but on recall at the
Youden-optimal threshold (the metric this paper otherwise
emphasizes), the naive single-node heuristic significantly
\emph{outperforms} PageRank, while PageRank in turn significantly
outperforms uniform weighting, a ranking that holds in every one of
the five paired folds without exception.  We report this as a genuine
finding rather than reporting only the metric more favorable to the
artifact: on this benchmark, the specific contribution of
PageRank-based node weighting to the graph score's headline recall
number is negative relative to the simplest possible alternative,
even though PageRank is not simply inert (it does outperform uniform
weighting on both metrics).  This qualifies TC2's relational-view
claim: the relational \emph{structure} encoded in the graph's edges
and $z$-scores appears to carry the paper's empirical advantage, while
the specific choice to propagate that structure through PageRank,
rather than a cruder importance measure, does not.

\subsection*{Future Research Directions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal graph dynamics.}  Extending to multi-period graphs
    that capture year-over-year changes in edge strain to detect
    \emph{deteriorating} relationship patterns.

    \item \textbf{Graph neural networks.}  Replacing handcrafted graph metrics
    with end-to-end learned GNN representations
    \citep{liu2020,wang2019}.

    \item \textbf{Cross-jurisdictional validation.}  Testing on Chinese listed
    firms, European IFRS filings, and emerging-market registrants to assess
    generalizability \citep{ho2015}.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble weight optimization.}  Replacing the \textit{a
    priori} weights with data-driven Bayesian optimization or stacking.

    \item \textbf{Supply-chain network effects.}  Extending the vulnerability
    graph to model multi-firm relationships and manipulation propagation across
    supply chains \citep{savage2014}.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{\centering VII. CONCLUSION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}
% =============================================================================

This paper presented ARS-VG, a Design Science Research artifact that
provides an analytical framework for observing and classifying
AEM--REM substitution at the individual-firm level.  Grounded in
agency theory and the substitution hypothesis, ARS-VG models financial
statements as directed graphs and operationalizes governance-moderated
substitution classification, demonstrated on four theoretically
calibrated scenarios.

DSR evaluation on the JarFraud benchmark ($N = 146{,}045$; 0.7 percent
fraud rate) provides evidence that the graph component captures
complementary relational signals that are not fully reflected in
traditional parametric models, achieving the highest Youden-optimal
recall among all four methods tested
(77.1 percent for the Ensemble, versus 67.4 percent for Dechow, 60.6 percent for
Beneish, and 57.1 percent for \citet{bao2020}'s 28-feature RUSBoost).  We make no
claim of ranking dominance: the graph layer's aggregate AUC is modest
(approximately 0.55), indicating limited ranking power relative to
parametric and machine learning baselines, Bao's RUSBoost dominates at
strict low-flag-rate operating points, and ARS-VG's precision at the
reported Youden threshold is not audit-ready.  A feature-asymmetry
decomposition attributes roughly three-quarters of Bao's aggregate AUC
advantage to richer feature access rather than non-linear learning,
pricing the interpretability cost of channel-level aggregation at
$0.194$ AUC on this benchmark, the paper's most fully verified
empirical measurement, independent of the limitations below, though
whether it counts as evidence for or against the artifact's design is
a matter of interpretation rather than a settled fact (Section~VI).
Scenario testing verifies correct rule-execution on all four canonical
manipulation regimes, including a governance-triggered substitution
case, though this confirms the classifier implements its encoded
rules correctly rather than independently validating that it detects
substitution in data external to those rules.  Population-scale
evaluation of the substitution classifier does
not succeed: under JarFraud's necessarily covariate-sparse governance
specification, the classifier's decision rule is arithmetically
unreachable, a property we verify analytically rather than merely
observe empirically.  We report this as a substantive finding in its
own right, a general caution for discrete, threshold-gated governance
classifiers deployed under similar data constraints, rather than
omitting it or reporting it as a weaker positive result than the
evidence supports.  A separate ablation isolates whether PageRank-weighted
node importance, specifically, contributes to the graph score's
performance: it does not improve recall (the metric this paper
emphasizes) relative to a naive single-node heuristic, though it
does outperform uniform node weighting on both AUC and recall, a
pattern that holds consistently across every cross-validation fold.
We report this alongside TC4 as a second fully tested empirical
result, rather than allowing the graph-theoretic framing to rest on
an untested assumption.  The artifact's primary contribution is therefore
not predictive superiority over gradient-boosted ML, nor a validated
population-scale substitution measure, but a demonstrated,
interpretable classification capability at the firm level together
with a precisely quantified interpretability-accuracy tradeoff and a
transparent account of what richer governance data would be required
to test the population-scale claim this paper does not make.

As governance pressure continues to drive the substitution of AEM for REM,
both researchers and practitioners need frameworks that model this phenomenon
at the firm level.  ARS-VG offers a theoretically grounded artifact and
a rigorously characterized account of its current evaluation boundary,
intended as a foundation for the governance-data extension identified
throughout this paper as a natural direction for future work.
