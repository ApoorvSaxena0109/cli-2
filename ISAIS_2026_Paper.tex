{\Large\textbf{ARS-VG: A Graph-Theoretic Intelligent Artifact for Joint
Detection of Accrual-Based and Real-Activities Earnings Management
Substitution in SEC-Registered Firms --- A Design Science Research
Approach}}
\end{center}
\thispagestyle{empty}

% --- Abstract ----------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Although the substitution between accrual-based earnings management (AEM)
and real activities manipulation (REM) is well documented at the aggregate
level, existing detection tools analyse each channel in isolation, leaving
firm-level substitution unobservable.  This paper presents
\textbf{ARS-VG} (Accrual--Real Substitution Vulnerability Graph), a Design
Science Research artifact that models financial statements as directed
weighted graphs, applies industry-relative $z$-score anomaly detection,
and incorporates governance constraints to classify substitution.
Evaluated on the JarFraud benchmark (146{,}045 firm-years) against the
Beneish M-Score, Dechow F-Score, and \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost, the
ensemble achieves the highest recall at the Youden-optimal point
(77.1 percent); RUSBoost dominates at strict operating points.  Panel-level
application reveals a statistically significant SOX structural break, and
construct validation against Richardson RSST accruals confirms that
flagged firms exhibit $2.7\times$ higher aggressive-accrual activity
($\rho = +0.156$).  ARS-VG is positioned as observational research
infrastructure for substitution research, not an audit-team screen.
\end{abstract}

\section*{\centering I. INTRODUCTION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Financial statement fraud remains one of the most costly and persistent
challenges in capital markets.  The Association of Certified Fraud
Examiners \citep{acfe2024} estimates that organisations lose approximately
5 percent of annual revenue to occupational fraud broadly defined, with
financial statement schemes---though less frequent than asset
misappropriation---producing the largest median losses per incident.  Despite advances in
auditing standards and regulatory oversight, high-profile scandals---from
Enron and WorldCom to more recent cases such as Wirecard---underscore the
difficulty of detecting earnings manipulation in a timely manner.

Academic research has responded with a rich portfolio of statistical models.
The Beneish M-Score \citep{beneish1999} uses financial-ratio analysis to flag
firms likely to have manipulated earnings through accrual-based techniques.
The Dechow F-Score \citep{dechow2011} incorporates accrual quality measures
derived from the Richardson et al.\ \citep{richardson2005} RSST accrual
decomposition, soft-asset ratios, and changes in receivables.  More recently,
machine learning methods---random forests, gradient boosting, neural
networks---have been applied to fraud detection with promising results
\citep{bao2020,cecchini2010,craja2020}.  However, these data-driven
approaches often function as ``black boxes,'' producing predictions without the
interpretable audit trail that practitioners require \citep{green1997}.

A critical gap in all these approaches is their treatment of financial accounts
as \emph{independent} variables.  In practice, financial statements are a
system of interconnected accounts governed by double-entry bookkeeping,
industry-specific operating cycles, and accrual accounting rules.  When a firm
manipulates earnings, the manipulation rarely distorts a single ratio in
isolation; instead, it creates \emph{strain} that propagates across related
accounts \citep{dechow1995}.  Revenue inflation through premature recognition
simultaneously distorts the revenue-to-receivables relationship (elevated days
sales outstanding), the revenue-to-cash-flow relationship (diverging operating
cash flows), and the accrual-to-income relationship (increasing discretionary
accruals).  Traditional methods may detect each symptom separately, but they
do not model the \emph{relational structure} that connects them.

This relational perspective is especially important in light of the
well-documented phenomenon of \emph{AEM--REM substitution}.
\citet{cohen2008} and \citet{zang2012} demonstrate that firms strategically
substitute between accrual-based earnings management and real activities
manipulation in response to varying governance constraints---audit quality,
litigation risk, institutional ownership, and regulatory scrutiny.  When
accrual manipulation becomes costly (e.g., after the Sarbanes--Oxley Act of
2002), firms shift toward real activities such as overproduction, discretionary
expense cuts, or channel stuffing \citep{roychowdhury2006}.  This substitution
is an inherently relational phenomenon: pressure that cannot flow through one
manipulation channel is redirected to another.  A detection system that
analyses each channel independently will miss the substitution pattern.

This motivates our central research question:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} How can firm-level AEM--REM substitution be
operationalized and observed using a graph-theoretic artifact, and how
does such an approach compare to established detection methods in
terms of recall, precision, and interpretability?
\end{quote}

We address this question through the \emph{Design Science Research} (DSR)
paradigm \citep{hevner2004,peffers2007}.  The primary objective of this study
is to design and evaluate a novel artifact---not to test empirical hypotheses
in the traditional sense.  The empirical evaluation that follows serves as
the DSR evaluation mechanism, demonstrating the artifact's utility relative
to established baselines rather than claiming statistical generalisation
about fraud incidence.

In this paper, we propose \textbf{ARS-VG}, a graph-theoretic intelligent
artifact developed through the DSR paradigm to address these limitations.  ARS-VG is not
merely a detection tool; it provides an \emph{analytical framework} that
enables researchers and practitioners to observe, measure, and classify
AEM--REM substitution at the individual-firm level---a capability that
existing aggregate-level approaches lack.  Our approach makes six
contributions---four theoretical (TC) and two practical (PC)---all of
which are supported by the deterministic graph and ensemble layers
of the artifact.  The artifact also includes a downstream
natural-language narrative-generation layer (Section~IV)
whose formal evaluation is scoped as a companion study; the empirical
results in Section~V are invariant to its presence.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{TC1}] \textbf{Firm-level substitution framework.}  ARS-VG
    operationalises the AEM--REM substitution hypothesis
    \citep{zang2012,cohen2008} as a firm-level scoring and classification
    system.  By jointly modelling both channels in a single vulnerability
    graph, the artifact enables a proxy-based firm-level classification
    of substitution patterns, thereby facilitating empirical analysis of
    substitution behavior that aggregate cross-sectional designs can only
    infer indirectly---responding to the measurement concerns raised by
    \citet{cohenlys2022} and the evidence of time-varying substitution
    documented by \citet{pincus2022}.

    \item[\textbf{TC2}] \textbf{Relational view of earnings management
    detection.}  Financial statements are modelled as directed graphs;
    deviations are quantified as \emph{edge strain} using industry-adjusted
    $z$-scores.  This relational perspective extends the earnings management
    detection literature beyond the ratio-decomposition paradigm, revealing
    how manipulation creates \emph{strain propagation} across connected
    accounts---a pattern invisible to methods that treat accounts as
    independent variables.

    \item[\textbf{TC3}] \textbf{Governance-moderated substitution:
    architecturally integrated; empirically tested on the SOX lever.}
    The substitution classifier integrates four governance constraints
    (auditor type, institutional ownership, board independence, and
    SOX compliance) into the substitution-classification stage,
    operationalising the agency-theoretic prediction \citep{jensen1976}
    that strong governance suppresses AEM while redirecting
    manipulation pressure to REM.  The SOX arm is tested empirically
    at panel scale on JarFraud (Section~V),
    yielding a statistically significant structural break.  The
    remaining three constraints (auditor, institutional ownership,
    board independence) are validated in the scenario tests
    (Section~V) but are not available as time-varying
    covariates in JarFraud; their full panel-scale test requires a
    Compustat/BoardEx merge that we identify as the primary follow-up
    and do not claim in the present paper.

    \item[\textbf{TC4}] \textbf{Quantified feature-aggregation cost of
    interpretability.}  By comparing a 28-feature gradient-boosted baseline
    \citep{bao2020} to the same algorithm restricted to ARS-VG's three
    channel-level aggregates, we decompose the aggregate AUC gap into a
    feature-access component ($+0.194$) and an algorithm-class component
    ($+0.065$).  This gives auditor-facing applications, in which
    channel-level decomposition is a requirement rather than a preference,
    a priceable tradeoff between raw-feature discrimination and
    interpretable aggregation.

    \item[\textbf{PC1}] \textbf{Research infrastructure for
    population-scale substitution analysis.}  The practical value of
    ARS-VG is as an observational tool for researchers studying
    AEM--REM substitution at the population level, not as a
    production fraud screen.  The 7.99 percent panel-level substitution
    flag rate, validated against a non-enforcement-based accrual
    proxy (Section~V), makes the flag usable
    as a dependent variable in panel studies of earnings management
    and as a descriptive diagnostic for industry-year substitution
    incidence.  It is \emph{not} an audit-team triage instrument:
    its 0.8 percent precision at the Youden operating point implies
    flagging on the order of ninety thousand firm-years to capture
    seven hundred true positives, which no audit team can follow up.
    At strict low-flag-rate operating points \citet{bao2020}'s
    RUSBoost dominates, and for audit-team deployment we recommend
    Bao's artifact over ours.

    \item[\textbf{PC2}] \textbf{Regulatory monitoring.}  SEC EDGAR integration
    enables scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant
    population without requiring proprietary data access.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~II develops the theoretical foundations.
Section~III describes the DSR methodology.
Section~IV presents the system design.
Section~V reports the empirical evaluation.
Section~VI discusses findings and contributions.
Section~VII concludes.

\section*{\centering II. THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:theory}
% =============================================================================

\subsection*{Agency Theory and Information Asymmetry}

The conceptual anchor of this study is agency theory
\citep{jensen1976,eisenhardt1989}, which models the corporation as a nexus of
contracts between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers).  Because
managers possess superior information about firm prospects and activities, they
can exploit this asymmetry to pursue self-interest---including meeting earnings
benchmarks to preserve compensation or job security
\citep{graham2005,healy1999}.  Governance mechanisms such as Big~4 audit
quality, board independence, institutional ownership, and analyst coverage are
theorised to reduce agency costs by constraining opportunistic reporting
\citep{shleifer1997,watts1986}.

Positive Accounting Theory \citep{watts1986} extends this framework by
predicting specific directions of earnings management: managers adopt
income-increasing strategies when approaching debt-covenant thresholds, when
bonus-plan targets are within reach, and when political scrutiny is low.  These
incentive structures interact with detection risk to determine whether a manager
will choose AEM, REM, or a combination of both \citep{graham2005}.

\subsection*{Accrual-Based Earnings Management}

AEM exploits discretion in accrual accounting to alter reported earnings
without changing underlying cash flows.  The seminal Jones \citeyearpar{jones1991}
model decomposes total accruals into normal (driven by operating conditions) and
discretionary components, with the latter serving as the manipulation proxy.
Subsequent refinements---the modified Jones model \citep{dechow1995} and the
performance-matched approach \citep{kothari2005}---improved specificity by
controlling for firm performance.  Key AEM indicators include abnormal
accounts-receivable growth, inventory build-up, and divergence between net
income and operating cash flow \citep{richardson2005}.

\subsection*{Real Activities Manipulation}

REM alters reported earnings through actions that affect actual cash flows and
operations.  \citet{roychowdhury2006} identified three primary REM channels:
(i)~abnormally high production to spread fixed costs and reduce cost of goods
sold, resulting in inflated inventory; (ii)~sales acceleration through channel
stuffing or price discounts, reducing cash flow from operations relative to
sales; and (iii)~cuts in discretionary expenditures such as R\&D, SG\&A, and
advertising.  \citet{gunny2010} demonstrates that firms engaging in REM to
just meet earnings benchmarks exhibit lower future operating performance,
confirming that REM imposes real economic costs.

\subsection*{The AEM--REM Substitution Hypothesis}

The most theoretically important development for the present work is the
documented substitution between AEM and REM.  \citet{cohen2008} documented a
structural break after SOX: AEM declined sharply while REM increased,
consistent with managers switching to harder-to-detect manipulation.
\citet{zang2012} formalised this as a sequential decision model: managers
choose REM first (because it is decided during the fiscal year) and use AEM as
a residual to achieve earnings targets---but only to the extent that audit
quality permits.  \citet{cohen2010} replicated the pattern around seasoned
equity offerings, while \citet{ho2015} and \citet{chi2011} showed that Big~4
audit quality suppresses AEM but increases REM.

More recently, \citet{pincus2022} re-examined the substitution effect over an
extended post-SOX period and found that the REM--AEM substitution pattern
weakens in later years, suggesting that the substitution dynamic may be
time-varying rather than monotonic.  \citet{cohenlys2022} provided further
commentary, questioning whether aggregate-level substitution measures
adequately capture firm-level strategic behaviour.  These findings do not
invalidate the substitution hypothesis; rather, they motivate a
\emph{firm-level} detection approach---such as ARS-VG---that can identify
substitution at the individual observation level rather than relying on
aggregate cross-sectional trends.

These findings imply that any single-channel detection approach will produce
systematically biased risk scores for firms under high governance pressure.  A
joint detection artifact that captures the \emph{relationship} between AEM and
REM signals is therefore both theoretically motivated and practically necessary.

\subsection*{Graph Analytics in Accounting Information Systems}

Graph-based methods have gained traction in financial applications, primarily
in money laundering detection \citep{savage2014}, insider trading networks
\citep{ahern2017}, and supply chain risk analysis.  More recently, graph neural
networks (GNNs) have been applied to fraud detection in transaction networks
\citep{liu2020,wang2019}.  \citet{caixie2024} proposed a two-layer knowledge graph (FSFD-TLKG) for
financial statement fraud detection, combining semantic and syntactic layers
to model relationships between financial variables.  While FSFD-TLKG also
operates at the firm level, it focuses on \emph{general} fraud pattern mining
without explicitly modelling the AEM--REM substitution dynamic or
incorporating governance constraints as a moderating mechanism.
Concurrently, large language models (LLMs) augmented with
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) have entered the auditing domain:
\citet{jetaaiagent2025} recently demonstrated an AI agent framework in JETA
that integrates RAG with audit methodologies.  However, these approaches
either mine general fraud patterns without theoretical grounding in the
substitution hypothesis, or apply AI to general audit workflows without
modelling specific manipulation channels.  Our vulnerability graph is,
to our knowledge, among the first to apply graph analytics to the
\emph{internal structure} of financial statements for earnings manipulation
detection, operationalising the relational view of accounting
\citep{dechow2011}.

the artifact itself and the theoretical insights it enables, not in the

evaluate, theorise) connect the knowledge base of financial accounting and

\subsection*{Problem Identification and Motivation}

As established in Section~II, no existing automated tool jointly
models AEM and REM or detects substitution patterns as filings become
available.  The
economic significance of this gap is confirmed by regulatory enforcement data:
the SEC's AAER database documents over 270 material-misstatement cases between
1982 and 2005, with earnings manipulation the predominant allegation
\citep{dechow2011}.  Existing tools address either relational modelling or
explainability, but none addresses both simultaneously---a design gap that
ARS-VG is specifically engineered to fill.

\subsection*{Objectives of a Solution}

The artifact must satisfy five design objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(O1)] Detect AEM signals with recall at least comparable to
    parametric baselines at the Youden-optimal operating point, and
    occupy a complementary region of the precision--recall frontier
    relative to gradient-boosted ML baselines.
    \item[(O2)] Detect REM signals using production, CFO, and discretionary-expenditure anomalies.
    \item[(O3)] Identify and classify AEM--REM substitution patterns.
    \item[(O4)] Expose the underlying edge-level anomalies and
    classification rules in human-readable form, so that each flag can
    be traced to its deterministic components.
    \item[(O5)] Allow interactive querying by CIK number via the SEC EDGAR API.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Design and Development}

ARS-VG was implemented in Python~3.11, leveraging NetworkX for graph
construction, ChromaDB for vector-store RAG, Ollama/DeepSeek-R1 for LLM
inference, and Gradio for the interactive interface.  Data and code
supporting the findings of this study, including the full implementation,
are available from the authors on reasonable request.  Section~IV details
the design decisions.

\subsection*{Demonstration}

The artifact was demonstrated on four synthetic but theoretically calibrated
scenarios (Section~V): a clean firm, an AEM-dominant
manipulator, an REM-dominant manipulator, and a substitution case with strong
governance.  All four scenarios produced the expected classification outcome
(pass rate = 100 percent), satisfying design objectives O1--O3.  Objective
O4 (edge-level human-readable exposure of flags) is implemented
natively in the deterministic graph and ensemble layers; a downstream
narrative-generation layer that surfaces these flags in natural
language is described in Section~IV with formal evaluation
scoped to a companion study.  Objective O5 (SEC EDGAR querying) is
implemented in the companion notebook with formal evaluation scoped
to a future practitioner-facing study.

\subsection*{Evaluation}

Utility was evaluated through: (i)~predictive accuracy on the JarFraud
panel under 5-fold stratified cross-validation
(Section~V); (ii)~comparison with four baselines---the
full-specification Beneish M-Score, Dechow F-Score,
\citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost ensemble on 28 raw Compustat features, and
the same RUSBoost algorithm restricted to the three aggregated ARS-VG
scores as a feature-asymmetry control; (iii)~component-level analysis
decomposing Bao's aggregate AUC advantage into feature-access and
algorithm-class contributions (Section~V);
(iv)~weight-simplex sensitivity for the ensemble at a matched flag
rate (Section~IV); and (v)~panel-level application of the
substitution classifier to test whether firm-level substitution is
detectable at population scale using the proposed classification
framework (Section~V).
All experiments are reported in the main text; raw per-fold outputs
and supplementary appendices are available from the authors on
reasonable request.

downstream natural-language narrative-generation layer described in
Section~IV---produces explainable narratives from the

\subsection*{Vulnerability Graph Construction}
\label{subsec:graph}

A firm's financial statements are modelled as a directed graph
$G = (V, E)$, where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nodes} $V = V_A \cup V_R \cup V_G$ comprise three types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Account nodes} ($V_A$): revenue, COGS, gross profit,
              operating income, net income, accounts receivable, inventory,
              accounts payable, PP\&E, and total assets.
        \item \emph{Ratio nodes} ($V_R$): DSO, DIO, DPO, and cash-conversion cycle.
        \item \emph{Governance nodes} ($V_G$): auditor type and auditor tenure.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Edges} $E$ connect nodes with known accounting relationships;
    each edge $e_{ij} \in E$ carries an expected ratio $\mu_{ij}$ and standard
    deviation $\sigma_{ij}$ estimated from cross-sectional firm-year
    distributions within each SIC group, using trimmed samples to mitigate
    the influence of outliers (Table~\ref{tab:edges}).
\end{itemize}

The graph deliberately focuses on a parsimonious set of accounting
relationships rather than enumerating all possible inter-account connections.
This design choice reflects two considerations.  First, the selected edges
correspond to the specific financial relationships that prior literature
identifies as diagnostically relevant for AEM and REM---revenue-to-receivables
\citep{beneish1999}, COGS-to-inventory \citep{roychowdhury2006}, and
income-to-cash-flow \citep{dechow2011}---ensuring that the graph structure is
\emph{theory-driven} rather than exhaustive.  Second, a parsimonious graph
preserves interpretability: each edge carries a clear economic meaning that
auditors can evaluate, whereas a dense graph would obscure the signal with
noise from uninformative relationships.  The graph-theoretic metrics
(PageRank, betweenness centrality) derive their analytical value from the
\emph{structure} of these relationships, not from sheer graph density.

For each edge, \emph{edge strain} is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Strain}(e_{ij}) = \frac{|r_{ij} - \mu_{ij}|}{\sigma_{ij}},
\label{eq:strain}
\end{equation}
where $r_{ij}$ is the observed ratio between source and target node values.
Edge strain exceeding 2.0 s.d.\ is classified as \textsc{broken}; between 1.0
and 2.0 as \textsc{stressed}; and below 1.0 as \textsc{intact}.  These
thresholds follow conventional one- and two-standard-deviation cutoffs;
we treat them as design parameters rather than empirically optimized
thresholds and assess robustness in sensitivity analyses.

\subsection*{Edge Anomaly Detection with Industry-Adjusted $z$-Scores}
\label{subsec:anomaly}

We apply industry-adjusted $z$-scores to four diagnostically critical edges
that capture both AEM and REM pathways:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (cash quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{CFO}} = \frac{(\text{CFO}/\text{Revenue}) - \mu_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where superscript $(k)$ denotes the SIC-based industry group.  A strongly
    negative $z_{\text{CFO}}$ signals revenue that does not convert to
    cash---a hallmark of premature revenue recognition (AEM).

    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Accounts Receivable} (collection quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DSO}} = \frac{\text{DSO} - \mu_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DSO} = (\text{AR}/\text{Revenue})\times 365$.  Elevated DSO
    suggests receivables growing faster than revenue---a channel-stuffing or
    fictitious-revenue signal.

    \item \textbf{COGS $\to$ Inventory} (production efficiency):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DIO}} = \frac{\text{DIO} - \mu_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DIO} = (\text{Inventory}/\text{COGS})\times 365$.  Elevated
    DIO signals overproduction to spread fixed costs---a classic REM technique
    \citep{roychowdhury2006}.

    \item \textbf{Net Income $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (accrual quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{Accrual}} = \frac{(\text{NI} - \text{CFO})/\text{TA} - \mu_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where TA denotes total assets.  A high positive $z_{\text{Accrual}}$
    indicates earnings far above cash generation---a direct AEM indicator.
\end{enumerate}

Industry benchmarks are specified for five broad SIC-based groups:
Technology (SIC 3500--3700, 7370--7379), Manufacturing (SIC 2000--3499),
Retail (SIC 5200--5999), Services (SIC 7000--8999 excluding technology),
and a Default category for all remaining sectors.
The benchmark values ($\mu$, $\sigma$) are derived from Compustat
cross-sectional medians and interquartile ranges for the respective
financial ratios, rounded to representative values for implementation.
Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} reports the benchmark parameters.

\subsection*{Graph-Theoretic Risk Metrics}

After computing edge-level anomalies, we derive three graph-theoretic metrics
using NetworkX:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PageRank} \citep{brin1998}: identifies nodes with highest
    importance in the risk-propagation network, weighted by edge strain.
    \item \textbf{Betweenness centrality} \citep{freeman1977}: identifies
    nodes that act as critical intermediaries through which manipulation strain
    must flow.
    \item \textbf{Aggregate node risk}: a weighted combination of adjacent
    edge strain:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Risk}(v)
        = 0.6\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\max_{e \ni v}\,\text{Strain}(e)}{3},\,1\right)
        + 0.4\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)}{2},\,1\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)$ is the mean strain of edges incident to
    node $v$.  The 0.6/0.4 weighting gives priority to peak strain over
    average strain, reflecting the forensic accounting intuition that a single
    severely broken relationship (e.g., revenue--receivables) is more
    diagnostic of manipulation than moderate strain spread across several
    edges \citep{beneish1999,dechow2011}.
\end{itemize}

The final \textbf{graph risk score} aggregates edge-level severities:
\begin{equation}
    \text{GraphRisk}
    = \min\!\!\left(1,\;
      0.35\!\times\!n_{\text{\textsc{broken}}}
      + 0.15\!\times\!n_{\text{\textsc{stressed}}}
      + \min\!\!\left(0.5,\;\frac{\overline{|z|}}{4}\right)\right),
    \label{eq:graphrisk}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{\textsc{broken}}}$ and $n_{\text{\textsc{stressed}}}$ count broken and
stressed edges among the four critical edges, and $\overline{|z|}$ is the mean
absolute $z$-score.  Broken edges ($>$2\,SD) receive roughly double the weight
of stressed edges (1--2\,SD) because a deviation exceeding two standard
deviations from industry norms indicates a qualitatively different severity
level that is more likely to reflect deliberate manipulation rather than
normal operating variation.

\subsection*{Traditional Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:traditional}

Two established methods are implemented for comparison and ensemble
integration.

\textbf{Beneish M-Score (full eight-variable specification) \citep{beneish1999}:}%
\footnote{All eight indices are computed.  Prior-year values are obtained by
a panel self-join on \texttt{(gvkey, fyear$-1$)} within JarFraud; for
firm-years where a matching prior observation is not present in the panel
(approximately one-quarter of the sample, concentrated at panel entry and
after acquisitions), we use an asymmetric-proxy lag that applies a 0.95
scaling factor to flow variables (sale, cogs, xsga, dp, lt, at, act, rect)
and the identity to stock variables that are near-invariant period-over-period
(ppegt).  This asymmetry preserves the ratio-of-ratio signal that symmetric
scaling would cancel out.  Cash flow from operations is approximated as
$\mathrm{CFO} = \mathrm{NI} + \mathrm{DP}$ because the JarFraud panel does
not carry \texttt{oancf}.  Intercept and coefficients are taken directly
from \citet{beneish1999}; no re-estimation is performed on the evaluation
sample.}
\begin{align}
    M = \; &-4.84
      + 0.920\!\times\!\mathrm{DSRI}
      + 0.528\!\times\!\mathrm{GMI}
      + 0.404\!\times\!\mathrm{AQI}
      + 0.892\!\times\!\mathrm{SGI} \notag \\
     &+ 0.115\!\times\!\mathrm{DEPI}
      - 0.172\!\times\!\mathrm{SGAI}
      + 4.679\!\times\!\mathrm{TATA}
      - 0.327\!\times\!\mathrm{LVGI},
\end{align}
where each index is the Beneish (1999) ratio-of-ratios (DSRI: days' sales
in receivables; GMI: gross margin; AQI: asset quality; SGI: sales growth;
DEPI: depreciation; SGAI: SG\&A; TATA: total accruals over total assets;
LVGI: leverage).  The fraud probability is
$P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-(M+1.78)})$.

\textbf{Dechow F-Score (full specification) \citep{dechow2011}:}%
\footnote{All seven Dechow et al.\ (2011) Model~1 predictors plus the
securities-issuance indicator are computed directly from JarFraud's
pre-computed lagged-differential fields (\texttt{ch\_rsst}, \texttt{dch\_rec},
\texttt{dch\_inv}, \texttt{soft\_assets}, \texttt{dch\_cs}, \texttt{dch\_cm},
\texttt{dch\_roa}, \texttt{issue}).  Because \citet{bao2020} packaged these
lagged features into the JarFraud panel, no additional Compustat merge is
required to recover them.  Intercept and coefficients are taken from the
original \citet{dechow2011} Table~7 estimates; no re-estimation is performed
on the evaluation sample.}
\begin{align}
    F = \; &-7.893
      + 0.790\!\times\!\mathrm{RSST}
      + 2.518\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{Rec}
      + 1.191\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{Inv}
      + 1.979\!\times\!\mathrm{SoftAssets} \notag \\
     &+ 0.171\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{CashSales}
      - 0.932\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{CashMargin}
      + 1.029\!\times\!\Delta\mathrm{ROA}
      + 0.149\!\times\!\mathrm{Issue},
\end{align}
where RSST denotes \citet{richardson2005} accruals, the $\Delta$ terms are
year-over-year changes in receivables, inventories, cash sales, cash margin,
and ROA, SoftAssets is the soft-asset ratio, and $\mathrm{Issue}$ is a
binary indicator for debt or equity issuance during the fiscal year.  The
fraud probability is $P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-F})$.

\subsection*{Ensemble Method}
\label{subsec:ensemble}

Because the graph score captures relational signals while the traditional
scores capture ratio-level anomalies, we combine the three detection scores
in a weighted ensemble to exploit their complementarity:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Ensemble} = 0.25\!\times\!\hat{B}
                    + 0.35\!\times\!\hat{D}
                    + 0.40\!\times\!G,
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{B}$ is the min-max normalised Beneish probability, $\hat{D}$ is
the Dechow F-Score probability, and $G$ is the graph risk score from
Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk}.  The weights are set \textit{a priori} based
on conceptual considerations and are subsequently evaluated through a
comprehensive weight-simplex sensitivity analysis: the graph component receives the
highest weight (0.40) because it captures relational information absent from
the other two methods; the Dechow F-Score (0.35) is the stronger traditional
baseline in prior literature; the Beneish M-Score (0.25) provides a
complementary legacy signal.  These weights reflect a principled design
choice rather than a data-driven optimisation, so their calibration consumes
no degrees of freedom from the evaluation sample.  We acknowledge, however,
that the \textit{a priori} weight specification is itself a free-parameter
choice that warrants empirical robustness testing.  We conduct a formal
sensitivity analysis over the $(\hat{B}, \hat{D}, G)$ weight simplex,
varying each weight on a 0.05 grid under the constraint that weights sum
to one (231 combinations) and re-computing fold-pooled recall at a fixed
5 percent flag rate.  Recall ranges from 4.0 percent (minimum of the simplex) to
12.4 percent (maximum); the headline weights yield 4.7 percent, the Dechow-only
reference at the same flag rate is 11.7 percent, and only 1.3 percent of the
weight simplex achieves recall at or above the Dechow-only benchmark.
The weight choice is therefore load-bearing: the ensemble's advantage
over Dechow materialises at the permissive Youden operating point but
largely disappears at strict operating points regardless of weight
selection (full table available from the authors on reasonable request).
Data-driven weight selection via Bayesian optimisation or stacking over
cross-validation folds is identified as a natural extension.
Classification thresholds are optimised per fold using Youden's $J$
statistic.

\subsection*{Downstream Narrative-Generation Layer}
\label{subsec:xai}

The artifact includes a downstream narrative-generation layer that
translates the rule-based outputs of the graph and ensemble layers
into natural-language summaries for use in the demonstration
interface.  Case retrieval is implemented via a ChromaDB vector store
of historical SEC AAER descriptions and academic fraud-pattern
writeups, embedded with Sentence-Transformers \citep{reimers2019};
narrative synthesis is implemented via a locally deployed
open-source generative model (DeepSeek-R1, \citealt{guo2025}, served
through Ollama).  The layer is architecturally downstream of all
detection and classification logic: the graph, ensemble, and
substitution-classifier outputs reported in Section~V are
invariant to its presence.  Formal evaluation of the narrative
layer---hallucination benchmarking, practitioner-judgement
agreement, downstream audit-task impact, and the regulatory-
explainability requirements of the EU AI Act and analogous
frameworks \citep{euaiact2024}---is the scope of a companion study.

\subsection*{AEM--REM Substitution Classification}
\label{subsec:subst}

The governance constraint score $G_C$---aggregating auditor quality, board
independence, institutional ownership, SOX compliance, and analyst coverage---
mediates the AEM--REM balance.  A substitution pattern (AEM$\to$REM) is flagged
when:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{AEM}} > \theta_{\text{AEM}},\quad
    S_{\text{REM}} > \theta_{\text{REM}},\quad
    G_C > \bar{G}_C,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{G}_C$ is the sample median governance score, and thresholds are
set at $\theta_{\text{AEM}} = 0.30$ and $\theta_{\text{REM}} = 0.40$,
corresponding to the 70th percentile of the respective score distributions
across training folds.

A second, governance-triggered branch flags substitution when governance is
strong ($G_C > \bar{G}_C$) and the REM score exceeds its threshold
($S_{\text{REM}} > \theta_{\text{REM}}$), even if the AEM score falls below
$\theta_{\text{AEM}}$.  This branch operationalises the prediction that strong
governance \emph{suppresses} AEM below its normal detection level while
redirecting manipulation pressure to REM---exactly the balloon-effect pattern
described by \citeauthor{zang2012} \citeyearpar{zang2012}.

\subsection*{Dataset}

We use the JarFraud dataset \citep{bao2020}, comprising 146{,}045 firm-year
observations for U.S.\ SEC registrants spanning 1991--2014.  Binary fraud
labels are derived from SEC Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Releases
(AAERs), with AAER coverage most complete through 2008; post-2008
observations carry substantially fewer confirmed labels due to enforcement
lag.  Of the full panel, 964 firm-years (0.7 percent) carry confirmed fraud
labels.  Financial variables are sourced from Compustat via the JarFraud
panel.  No industry exclusions are applied; all SIC sectors are retained to
preserve population representativeness.

An important conceptual note: the theoretical framework of this paper
concerns \emph{earnings management} (both legal and aggressive), while the
JarFraud labels identify \emph{fraud} (material misstatements subject to SEC
enforcement).  AAER-labelled fraud represents the most extreme end of the
earnings management spectrum, implying that the evaluation focuses on
detecting extreme manifestations of manipulation rather than the full
spectrum of earnings management behavior.  We use AAER fraud labels as an empirical proxy
because they are the only large-scale, externally validated binary labels
available for earnings manipulation research.  The graph-based anomaly
detection is designed to capture the \emph{relational strain} that
characterises manipulation broadly, of which enforcement-level fraud is the
observable subset.  This construct gap has two implications for
interpretation.  First, the low precision (0.8 percent) overstates the
``false-positive'' rate: some flagged firms may engage in aggressive but
non-fraudulent earnings management that produces relational strain
detectable by the graph yet falls below the SEC enforcement threshold.
Second, recall is measured only against the observable fraud subset;
the artifact's sensitivity to milder manipulation cannot be assessed
with AAER labels alone and remains a direction for future research using
discretionary-accrual proxies or restatement databases.

\subsubsection*{Operating-point profile}
\label{subsec:operating-point}

Aggregate AUC comparisons obscure a first-order distinction in where the
methods add value.  At strict operating points the ordering is Bao
$>$ Dechow $>$ Ensemble: RUSBoost's recall at a 5 percent flag rate is
28.7 percent, while Dechow achieves 11.7 percent and the ARS-VG Ensemble 4.0 percent.
At the Youden-optimal threshold, which is permissive enough to flag on
the order of half the panel, the ordering reverses for recall: Ensemble
77.1 percent $>$ Dechow 67.4 percent $>$ RUSBoost 57.1 percent.  The artifact therefore
occupies a distinct region of the operating curve---the high-recall,
high-flag end---that is structurally different from the discrimination
frontier explored by gradient-boosted tree ensembles.  Which operating
point is appropriate depends on downstream use: an audit team with finite
triage capacity will prefer Bao; a researcher or regulator screening
for \emph{substitution-consistent} firms at population scale will find
the Ensemble's operating profile more useful.

\subsubsection*{Feature asymmetry: decomposing Bao's advantage}
\label{subsec:feature-asymmetry}

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:results} is not a like-for-like contrast
of algorithmic sophistication.  ARS-VG's Ensemble is a linear combination
of three aggregated, theoretically interpretable scalars
$\{\hat{B}, \hat{D}, G\}$; \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost is a gradient-boosted
ensemble over 28 raw Compustat variables.  The two methods differ in
algorithm class \emph{and} in feature access.  To isolate which factor
drives the AUC gap, we retrain RUSBoost using the same hyperparameters
and the same cross-validation folds, but restricted to the three
aggregated scores.  The result (Table~\ref{tab:results}, second-to-last
row) is AUC $=0.618$: a $+0.065$ gain over the linear Ensemble
($0.553$), and a $-0.194$ shortfall relative to the 28-feature
configuration ($0.812$).  Roughly three-quarters of Bao's aggregate
advantage on JarFraud is therefore attributable to richer feature
access, not to non-linear learning per se.

This decomposition has both a defensive and a constructive implication.
Defensively, it contextualises the AUC gap: ARS-VG is not a weaker
detector because graph-theoretic methods are inherently less powerful
than gradient boosting; it is a less discriminating ranker on JarFraud
primarily because its inputs are three interpretable aggregates rather
than twenty-eight raw ledger items.  Constructively, it prices the
specific interpretability choice this artifact embodies: moving from
\citet{bao2020}'s 28-feature Compustat specification to ARS-VG's three
channel-level aggregates costs $0.194$ AUC on JarFraud under an
identical RUSBoost pipeline.  We do not claim this is a per-feature
constant that generalises to other feature sets or other panels---the
quantity depends on which 28 features are available and which
three aggregates are chosen, and a linear extrapolation is not
supported by the two-point measurement we report---only that on this
specific benchmark the feature-aggregation cost is a definable
quantity that practitioners can weigh against the interpretability
and channel-decomposition properties the aggregation enables, rather
than a latent confound in method comparisons.

\textbf{Reading the AUC honestly.}  An AUC of $0.550$ indicates that the
graph risk score has very little ranking power across the full score
distribution.  Part of this is structural---the graph risk score
(Equation~\ref{eq:graphrisk}) is a bounded, non-probabilistic aggregate
that compresses discrimination across the score range, whereas the Dechow
F-Score is a smooth parametric probability---but we do not attempt to
argue that the bounded construction \emph{explains away} the low AUC.
The graph layer is informative at the Youden operating point and not
much beyond it; it is not a standalone ranker, and it should not be
interpreted as one.  The ensemble's recall gain of 9.7 percentage points
over Dechow (77.1 percent vs.\ 67.4 percent) represents fraud firm-years that the
Dechow F-Score alone fails to flag---cases where manipulation creates
relational strain across connected accounts rather than isolated ratio
anomalies---and this complementarity, rather than ranking superiority,
is what motivates the ensemble.

\textbf{Reading the precision honestly.}  At a 0.7 percent base rate and
$N = 146{,}045$ firm-years, a Youden-optimal threshold that achieves
75--77 percent recall necessarily flags on the order of ninety thousand
firm-years to capture roughly seven hundred true fraud cases.  This is
not an audit-ready screen.  No audit team can follow up on ninety thousand
flags, and the \citet{karpoff2008} asymmetric-cost argument---that firms
lose \$3.08 in market value for every \$1 of fraudulent inflation revealed,
which superficially favours high-recall screening---does not by itself
rescue an operating point at this density of alerts.  We therefore
position ARS-VG, in its current form, as a \emph{research prototype} whose
practical deployment requires one of three forms of further calibration
beyond the scope of this paper: (i) operating at a substantially stricter
threshold and reporting the full precision--recall curve rather than a
single Youden point; (ii) embedding the graph score in a multi-stage
triage ensemble in which downstream stages raise precision; or
(iii) reserving the graph score for the relational substitution-
classification function (Section~IV) for which it is uniquely
suited and using parametric scores for absolute risk ranking.  A full
precision--recall curve over stricter thresholds is available from the
authors on reasonable request; the head-to-head comparison
against \citet{bao2020} is reported in Table~\ref{tab:results} and
Section~V.

Temporal validation (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}) shows that all four
methods generalise to the held-out 2006--2014 period with less than
10 percent AUC change, confirming robustness beyond the training window.

\subsection*{Panel-Level Substitution Analysis}
\label{subsec:panel-substitution}

To test TC1 empirically---that firm-level AEM$\to$REM substitution is
\emph{observable} in realised data and not merely a demonstrable
architectural feature---we apply the substitution classifier to all
146{,}045 firm-years in the JarFraud panel.  Because JarFraud does not
carry the auditor, institutional-ownership, or board-independence
variables required for the full governance-moderated branch, we hold
those three inputs at neutral median defaults (Non-Big4, 50 percent
institutional ownership, 0.5 board independence) and activate SOX
compliance as the only time-varying governance lever, setting it to
\textit{true} for firm-years with fiscal year $\geq 2003$ and
\textit{false} otherwise.  The analysis reported here therefore tests
the SOX arm of TC3 on JarFraud; the full governance interaction is
deferred to a future study that merges JarFraud onto Compustat/BoardEx.

Three observations warrant discussion.

\emph{First, substitution is detectable at population scale using the
proposed classification framework.}  The classifier flags 7.99 percent of
firm-years in the panel.  This is a
non-trivial incidence for a mechanism that aggregate-level studies can
only infer indirectly and provides evidence consistent with TC1:
substitution is a firm-level, not merely firm-type-level, phenomenon
that can be detected in archival data.

\emph{Second, the SOX structural break is statistically significant but
its direction is complicated by anticipatory dynamics.}  The
two-proportion test rejects equality of pre- and post-SOX flag rates
($z = +5.64$, $p < 10^{-7}$), consistent with the substitution
hypothesis of \citet{cohen2008} and \citet{zang2012}.  The year-by-year
trajectory, however, shows that the flag rate rose gradually throughout
the 1990s, peaked at 12.1 percent in fiscal 2002---the year SOX was enacted
but before it bound---and then \emph{declined} to roughly 8 percent over
2003--2014.  The aggregate pre/post comparison is positive primarily
because early-1990s flag rates were very low.  We interpret this as
evidence that substitution behaviour responded to the pre-SOX
regulatory tightening and heightened enforcement environment of
1998--2002 rather than to SOX implementation \emph{per se}, which is
consistent with the anticipatory character of governance-driven
behaviour documented by \citet{pincus2022}.  The paper does not claim
a clean ``balloon effect'' discontinuity at 2003; the observed pattern
is more gradualist.

\emph{Third, the substitution flag does not predict AAER fraud.}  The
$\phi$-correlation between the binary substitution flag and the AAER
misstatement label is $-0.006$; flagged firms carry a slightly lower
AAER rate (0.50 percent) than unflagged firms (0.67 percent).  This is the
construct-gap phenomenon anticipated in Section~V (Dataset):
AAER captures fraud severe enough to trigger SEC enforcement, whereas
the substitution classifier is designed to flag aggressive-but-not-
necessarily-fraudulent real-activities manipulation, which the
enforcement record systematically under-represents.  The absence of a
positive flag--AAER association is therefore consistent with the
interpretation that the substitution flag captures broader earnings
management behavior beyond enforcement-identified fraud; it does,
however, foreclose using the substitution flag as a standalone fraud
screen and reinforces the artifact's positioning as an observational
tool rather than a predictive one.

\emph{Fourth, the substitution flag does correlate positively and
significantly with a non-enforcement-based earnings-management proxy.}
To test whether the null flag--AAER correlation reported above reflects
a flag that is tracking noise rather than aggressive-but-non-fraudulent
earnings management, we validate the flag against the Richardson RSST
accrual measure ($|\text{ch\_rsst}|$) included natively in JarFraud.
RSST is a non-enforcement-based aggregate accrual construct and is
standard in the earnings-management literature
\citep{richardson2005,dechow2011}.  The results
(Table~\ref{tab:construct-validation}) are unambiguous in direction: the
point-biserial correlation between the binary substitution flag and
$|\text{RSST}|$ is $\rho = +0.156$ ($p < 10^{-10}$,
$n = 141{,}194$ firm-years with non-missing RSST), the median
$|\text{RSST}|$ in the flagged subsample is $0.240$ versus $0.088$ in
the unflagged subsample (a $2.7\times$ elevation), and both the
one-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test and the two-sided Welch $t$-test
($t = +44.7$) reject equality at $p \approx 0$.  The rank correlation
between the continuous REM score and $|\text{RSST}|$ is $+0.267$
($p \approx 0$), consistent with real-activities overproduction
manifesting as inventory-side accrual inflation in the RSST
decomposition.

\section*{\centering VI. DISCUSSION}

\label{sec:discuss}

\subsection*{Theoretical Contributions}

\textbf{TC1 --- Firm-level substitution framework.}  ARS-VG is, to our
knowledge, the first computational artifact to operationalise the AEM--REM
substitution hypothesis as a firm-level scoring and classification system.
By jointly modelling both channels in a shared graph, the artifact makes
substitution \emph{observable} at the individual-firm level---enabling
direct empirical examination of a phenomenon that aggregate cross-sectional
designs can only infer indirectly.  This addresses the methodological
concerns raised by \citet{cohenlys2022}, who question whether aggregate
substitution measures adequately capture firm-level strategic behaviour,
and the time-varying dynamics documented by \citet{pincus2022}.

\textbf{TC2 --- Relational view of earnings management detection.}  The
application of graph-theoretic strain propagation to financial-statement
structures extends the earnings management detection literature beyond the
ratio-decomposition paradigm.  The graph perspective reveals that
manipulation creates multi-account relational strain---a pattern invisible
to methods that treat financial variables as independent---aligning with
\citet{march1995}'s observation that IS artifacts often produce theoretical
insights unavailable through conventional analytical methods.

\textbf{TC3 --- Governance-moderated substitution: architecturally
integrated, SOX-arm tested.}  The substitution classifier integrates
four governance constraints---auditor type, institutional ownership,
board independence, and SOX compliance---into the
substitution-classification stage, operationalising the
agency-theoretic prediction \citep{jensen1976} that strong governance
suppresses AEM while redirecting manipulation to REM.  Of the four
constraints, only SOX compliance is derivable from fiscal-year data
available in JarFraud; the other three require firm-year governance
panels that JarFraud does not carry.  We therefore test TC3 in two
forms.  Architecturally, all four constraints are validated by the
four scenario tests in Section~V, which confirm correct
regime classification under each governance configuration.
Empirically, the SOX arm is tested on all 146{,}045 JarFraud
firm-years (Section~V) and yields a
statistically significant structural break ($z = +5.64$,
$p < 10^{-7}$), though the pre-2002 peak indicates that the response
was anticipatory rather than discontinuous at SOX implementation.  A
full panel-scale test of the remaining three arms requires a
Compustat/BoardEx merge that is outside the scope of this paper and
is identified as the primary empirical follow-up.  We therefore claim
TC3 as an architectural contribution with partial empirical support,
not as a fully validated governance-regime test.

\textbf{TC4 --- Quantified feature-aggregation cost of interpretability.}
A recurring concern in accounting-AI research is that the tradeoff
between black-box predictive accuracy and interpretable aggregated
signals is discussed qualitatively but rarely priced.  The
feature-asymmetry decomposition reported in
Section~V gives a concrete price for this
tradeoff on a standard benchmark: holding algorithm class constant,
aggregating from twenty-eight raw Compustat features to three channel-
level scalars costs $0.194$ AUC on JarFraud.  Holding feature set
constant, moving from a linear ensemble to RUSBoost buys $0.065$ AUC.
This decomposition provides a priceable quantity that practitioners
and researchers can weigh against the interpretability and
channel-decomposition properties the aggregation enables, rather than
leaving the tradeoff as a latent confound in method comparisons.

\subsection*{Practical Contributions}

\textbf{PC1 --- Research infrastructure for population-scale
substitution analysis.}  The practical contribution of ARS-VG is
\emph{not} audit-team deployment.  At the Youden operating point the
ensemble achieves the highest recall of any tested method, but it
does so by flagging a share of the panel that no audit team can
triage; at strict low-flag-rate operating points \citet{bao2020}'s
RUSBoost dominates on precision and recall alike and is the
appropriate instrument for auditor-facing use.  Instead, the
practical value of ARS-VG is as observational research
infrastructure: the substitution flag, validated against the
Richardson RSST accrual measure
(Table~\ref{tab:construct-validation}), provides a firm-year
dependent variable suitable for panel studies of earnings management,
industry-year diagnostics of substitution incidence, and hypothesis
tests about governance--substitution interactions of the kind
envisaged in TC3.  Deployment as a fraud-detection screen is not the
use case we support and is not what the empirical evidence
warrants.

\textbf{PC2 --- Regulatory monitoring.}  The SEC EDGAR integration enables
scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant population without
requiring proprietary data access.

\textbf{Downstream narrative-generation layer.}  The artifact also
includes a downstream narrative-generation layer
(Section~IV) that translates detection outputs into
natural-language summaries via retrieval-augmented generation over a
vector store of historical fraud cases.  The layer is architecturally
separate from the detection and classification pipeline, and the
empirical claims in Section~V are invariant to its presence.
A dedicated evaluation---spanning hallucination measurement,
practitioner-judgement comparison, and downstream audit
outcomes---is the scope of a companion study.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Six limitations bound the current findings and warrant explicit
discussion.

\emph{First, ARS-VG does not dominate modern supervised machine-learning
fraud detectors on aggregate ranking.}  We report the head-to-head
comparison against \citet{bao2020}'s RUSBoost ensemble on the same
5-fold splits in Table~\ref{tab:results}: Bao achieves AUC $0.812$
versus the ARS-VG Ensemble's $0.553$, a $+0.259$ gap.  The
feature-asymmetry decomposition in Section~V
attributes approximately $+0.194$ of this gap to Bao's access to
twenty-eight raw Compustat features and only $+0.065$ to non-linear
learning; the remaining gap can reasonably be attributed to the
interpretability-driven choice to aggregate channel-level signals into
three scalars.  ARS-VG is therefore not a replacement for gradient-
boosted ML on absolute-risk ranking; it is a Design Science Research
artifact whose contribution is to make firm-level AEM--REM substitution
\emph{observable} while retaining channel-level interpretability.
Still unresolved are (i) whether the relational signal captured by the
graph is \emph{additive} to the signal captured by gradient-boosted
ensembles in a stacked configuration, (ii) whether adding transformer-
based text encoders over MD\&A filings \citep{craja2020} closes further
gaps, and (iii) whether a graph neural network operating on the same
28-feature panel would preserve interpretability without the
$0.194$-AUC aggregation cost.  These are the priority extensions.

\emph{Second, the panel-level substitution analysis (TC1 empirical test)
partially supports but does not cleanly confirm the balloon-effect
prediction.}  Results in Section~V show that the
substitution classifier flags 7.99 percent of firm-years and that the SOX
structural break is statistically significant ($z = +5.64$,
$p < 10^{-7}$).  However, the year-by-year trajectory shows a peak at
12.1 percent in 2002 \emph{before} SOX bound, and the post-2003 flag rate
declines to roughly 8 percent, suggesting substitution responded to the
pre-SOX regulatory environment rather than SOX implementation \emph{per
se}.  The substitution flag does not predict AAER misstatement
($\phi = -0.006$), but construct validation against the Richardson RSST
accrual measure (Table~\ref{tab:construct-validation}) confirms that
flagged firms exhibit $2.7\times$ higher median aggressive-accrual
activity than unflagged firms ($\rho = +0.156$, $p \approx 0$): the
flag is tracking an established earnings-management signal, not noise,
even though its scope differs from the enforcement-selected AAER
population.  A fully identified modified-Jones $|DA|$ study on
Compustat, together with restatement-database and comment-letter
cross-references, would strengthen this validation further and remains
a priority extension.  A full test of TC3---the governance-moderated
branch---requires merging JarFraud onto Compustat and BoardEx to
obtain firm-level auditor, institutional-ownership, and board-
independence variables; that merge is the second-priority extension.

\emph{Third, precision at the reported operating point is not
audit-ready.}  As discussed in Section~V, at a 0.7 percent base
rate a 75--77 percent recall implies flagging of order ninety thousand
firm-years to capture roughly seven hundred true positives.  The paper
therefore positions ARS-VG as a research prototype, not a deployable
screen, and identifies full PR-curve reporting and stricter operating
points as a required next step.

\emph{Fourth, AAER label scope and coverage.}  The JarFraud AAER labels
are most complete through 2008; performance on post-2008 reporting
environments, including IFRS-influenced periods, has not been tested
with comprehensive labels, and \citet{pincus2022}'s evidence that
substitution dynamics are time-varying warrants caution in generalising
to more recent periods.  Additionally, AAER captures only fraud severe
enough to trigger SEC enforcement; aggressive-but-legal earnings
management is not separately labelled, which biases the measured
precision downward.

\emph{Fifth, ensemble weights are set \textit{a priori} rather than
learned.}  The weights in Equation~\eqref{eq:ensemble} are chosen on
the basis of conceptual role, not fitted on the evaluation sample.
The weight-simplex sensitivity analysis reported in
Section~IV quantifies the consequence: at a matched
5 percent flag rate, recall varies from 4.0 percent to 12.4 percent across the
simplex, and only 1.3 percent of weight configurations meet or exceed the
Dechow-only recall benchmark (11.7 percent).  The weight choice is
therefore load-bearing in the strict-operating-point regime, though
it does not affect the qualitative ranking at the Youden point.
Data-driven weight selection via Bayesian optimisation, stacking, or
isotonic calibration over cross-validation folds is a natural
extension.

\emph{Sixth, formal evaluation of the downstream narrative-generation
layer is scoped to a companion study.}  The narrative layer described
in Section~IV is architecturally separate from the detection
and classification pipeline; none of the empirical results in
Section~V depends on it.  Hallucination benchmarking,
practitioner-judgement agreement, and downstream audit-task impact
require a study design distinct from the one pursued here and are
pursued separately.

\subsection*{Future Research Directions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal graph dynamics.}  Extending to multi-period graphs
    that capture year-over-year changes in edge strain to detect
    \emph{deteriorating} relationship patterns.

    \item \textbf{Graph neural networks.}  Replacing handcrafted graph metrics
    with end-to-end learned GNN representations
    \citep{liu2020,wang2019}.

    \item \textbf{Cross-jurisdictional validation.}  Testing on Chinese listed
    firms, European IFRS filings, and emerging-market registrants to assess
    generalisability \citep{ho2015}.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble weight optimisation.}  Replacing the \textit{a
    priori} weights with data-driven Bayesian optimisation or stacking.

    \item \textbf{Supply-chain network effects.}  Extending the vulnerability
    graph to model multi-firm relationships and manipulation propagation across
    supply chains \citep{savage2014}.

    \item \textbf{Formal evaluation of the auxiliary narrative layer.}
    The retrieval-augmented generation module described in
    Section~IV requires a dedicated study covering
    hallucination measurement, practitioner-judgement comparison,
    auditor-task impact, and the regulatory-explainability
    requirements of the EU AI Act and analogous frameworks
    \citep{euaiact2024}.  This is the scope of a separate paper, not
    a section within the present one.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{\centering VII. CONCLUSION}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}
% =============================================================================

This paper presented ARS-VG, a Design Science Research artifact that
provides an analytical framework for observing and classifying
AEM--REM substitution at the individual-firm level.  Grounded in
agency theory and the substitution hypothesis, ARS-VG models financial
statements as directed graphs, operationalises governance-moderated
substitution classification, and delivers a firm-year substitution
flag that can serve as observational research infrastructure for
population-scale studies of earnings management.

DSR evaluation on the JarFraud benchmark ($N = 146{,}045$; 0.7 percent
fraud rate) provides evidence that the graph component captures
complementary relational signals that are not fully reflected in
traditional parametric models, achieving the highest Youden-optimal
recall among all four methods tested
(77.1 percent for the Ensemble, versus 67.4 percent for Dechow, 60.6 percent for
Beneish, and 57.1 percent for \citet{bao2020}'s 28-feature RUSBoost).  We make no
claim of ranking dominance: the graph layer's aggregate AUC is modest
(approximately 0.55), indicating limited ranking power relative to
parametric and machine learning baselines, Bao's RUSBoost dominates at
strict low-flag-rate operating points, and ARS-VG's precision at the
reported Youden threshold is not audit-ready.  A feature-asymmetry decomposition attributes roughly
three-quarters of Bao's aggregate AUC advantage to richer feature
access rather than non-linear learning, pricing the interpretability
cost of channel-level aggregation at $0.194$ AUC.  Panel-level
application of the substitution classifier to all 146{,}045 JarFraud
firm-years flags 7.99 percent of observations and reveals a statistically
significant SOX structural break ($z = +5.64$, $p < 10^{-7}$), with an
anticipatory peak in 2002; external construct validation confirms that
flagged firms exhibit $2.7\times$ higher median Richardson-RSST
aggressive-accrual activity than unflagged firms
($\rho = +0.156$, $p \approx 0$), distinguishing the flag from noise
independently of AAER enforcement selection.  Scenario testing confirms
correct classification of all four canonical manipulation regimes,
including governance-triggered substitution.  The artifact's primary
contribution is not predictive superiority over gradient-boosted ML,
but the ability to make firm-level substitution patterns observable
and classifiable---a capability that aggregate-level approaches and
feature-level ML rankers alike lack.

As governance pressure continues to drive the substitution of AEM for REM,
both researchers and practitioners need frameworks that model this phenomenon
at the firm level.  ARS-VG offers a principled, theoretically grounded, and
empirically evaluated response to this need.
