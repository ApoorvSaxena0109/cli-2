\begin{abstract}
Earnings management remains a critical challenge in financial reporting.
Managers increasingly substitute between accrual-based earnings management
(AEM) and real activities manipulation (REM) in response to governance
constraints---the \emph{balloon effect} \citep{zang2012}.  Existing detection
tools analyse each manipulation channel in isolation, leaving a critical
gap that permits sophisticated firms to shift strategies undetected.
Grounded in agency theory \citep{jensen1976} and the AEM--REM substitution
hypothesis \citep{zang2012}, this paper presents \textbf{ARS-VG}
(Accrual--Real Substitution Vulnerability Graph), an intelligent
information-systems artifact designed and evaluated through the Design
Science Research paradigm \citep{hevner2004,peffers2007}.
ARS-VG models financial statements as directed weighted graphs, applies
industry-relative $z$-score anomaly detection, and augments its reasoning
with a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) large language model module.
Validation against the JarFraud dataset ($N = 146{,}045$ firm-years;
0.7\,\% fraud prevalence) shows that the graph component achieves
75.3\,\% recall---significantly higher than simplified Beneish (60.6\,\%)
and Dechow (67.4\,\%) proxies (McNemar $p < 0.001$)---with AUC-ROC of
0.550.  The weighted ensemble reaches 77.1\,\% recall (AUC-ROC 0.553).
Temporal validation (train: 1991--2002; test: 2003--2008) confirms
generalisation ($\leq 8\,\%$ AUC change).  Scenario testing correctly
classifies all four canonical manipulation regimes (pass rate = 100\,\%).
Three theoretical and three practical contributions extend the earnings
management detection literature.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Financial statement fraud remains one of the most costly and persistent
challenges in capital markets.  The Association of Certified Fraud
Examiners \citep{acfe2024} estimates that organisations lose approximately
5\,\% of annual revenue to occupational fraud, with financial statement fraud
producing the largest median losses per incident.  Despite advances in
auditing standards and regulatory oversight, high-profile scandals---from
Enron and WorldCom to more recent cases such as Wirecard---underscore the
difficulty of detecting earnings manipulation in a timely manner.

Academic research has responded with a rich portfolio of statistical models.
The Beneish M-Score \citep{beneish1999} uses financial-ratio analysis to flag
firms likely to have manipulated earnings through accrual-based techniques.
The Dechow F-Score \citep{dechow2011} incorporates accrual quality measures
derived from the Richardson et al.\ \citep{richardson2005} RSST accrual
decomposition, soft-asset ratios, and changes in receivables.  More recently,
machine learning methods---random forests, gradient boosting, neural
networks---have been applied to fraud detection with promising results
\citep{bao2020,cecchini2010,craja2020}.  However, these data-driven
approaches often function as ``black boxes,'' producing predictions without the
interpretable audit trail that practitioners require \citep{green1997}.

A critical gap in all these approaches is their treatment of financial accounts
as \emph{independent} variables.  In practice, financial statements are a
system of interconnected accounts governed by double-entry bookkeeping,
industry-specific operating cycles, and accrual accounting rules.  When a firm
manipulates earnings, the manipulation rarely distorts a single ratio in
isolation; instead, it creates \emph{strain} that propagates across related
accounts \citep{dechow1995}.  Revenue inflation through premature recognition
simultaneously distorts the revenue-to-receivables relationship (elevated days
sales outstanding), the revenue-to-cash-flow relationship (diverging operating
cash flows), and the accrual-to-income relationship (increasing discretionary
accruals).  Traditional methods may detect each symptom separately, but they
do not model the \emph{relational structure} that connects them.

This relational perspective is especially important in light of the
well-documented phenomenon of \emph{AEM--REM substitution}.
\citet{cohen2008} and \citet{zang2012} demonstrate that firms strategically
substitute between accrual-based earnings management and real activities
manipulation in response to varying governance constraints---audit quality,
litigation risk, institutional ownership, and regulatory scrutiny.  When
accrual manipulation becomes costly (e.g., after the Sarbanes--Oxley Act of
2002), firms shift toward real activities such as overproduction, discretionary
expense cuts, or channel stuffing \citep{roychowdhury2006}.  This substitution
is an inherently relational phenomenon: pressure that cannot flow through one
manipulation channel is redirected to another.  A detection system that
analyses each channel independently will miss the substitution pattern.

This motivates our central research question:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ:} How can a graph-theoretic information-systems artifact jointly
detect AEM and REM substitution patterns, and how does such an artifact
compare to established single-channel detection methods in terms of recall,
precision, and explainability?
\end{quote}

In this paper, we propose \textbf{ARS-VG}, a graph-theoretic intelligent
artifact developed through the Design Science Research (DSR) paradigm
\citep{hevner2004,peffers2007} to address these limitations.  Our approach
makes six contributions---three theoretical (TC) and three practical (PC):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{TC1}] \textbf{Joint AEM--REM operationalisation.}  While prior
    work has empirically documented AEM--REM trade-offs
    \citep{zang2012,badertscher2011}, ARS-VG is, to our knowledge, the first
    \emph{computational artifact} to operationalise the substitution hypothesis
    as a real-time, firm-level scoring system, jointly modelling both channels
    in a single unified vulnerability graph.

    \item[\textbf{TC2}] \textbf{Graph-theoretic earnings management detection.}
    Financial statements are modelled as directed graphs; deviations are
    quantified as \emph{edge strain} using industry-adjusted $z$-scores.
    Graph-theoretic metrics---PageRank, betweenness centrality, and path-based
    strain propagation---identify accounts most central to manipulation patterns.

    \item[\textbf{TC3}] \textbf{Governance as a detection moderator.}  By
    explicitly incorporating governance constraints (auditor type, institutional
    ownership, board independence) into the substitution-classification stage,
    ARS-VG operationalises agency-theoretic predictions directly in a detection
    architecture.

    \item[\textbf{PC1}] \textbf{Audit planning tool.}  The high recall of the
    graph component makes ARS-VG suitable for first-pass risk screening,
    directing engagement resources to accounts with anomalous graph centrality.

    \item[\textbf{PC2}] \textbf{Regulatory monitoring.}  SEC EDGAR integration
    enables scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant
    population without requiring proprietary data access.

    \item[\textbf{PC3}] \textbf{Explainable AI layer.}  A local large language
    model (DeepSeek-R1) augmented with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
    over historical SEC enforcement cases produces natural-language audit
    narratives, addressing the black-box limitation of purely quantitative
    detection approaches.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} develops the theoretical foundations.
Section~\ref{sec:dsr} describes the DSR methodology.
Section~\ref{sec:design} presents the system design.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the empirical evaluation.
Section~\ref{sec:discuss} discusses findings and contributions.
Section~\ref{sec:conclude} concludes.

\section{Theoretical Foundations}
\label{sec:theory}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Agency Theory and Information Asymmetry}

The conceptual anchor of this study is agency theory
\citep{jensen1976,eisenhardt1989}, which models the corporation as a nexus of
contracts between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers).  Because
managers possess superior information about firm prospects and activities, they
can exploit this asymmetry to pursue self-interest---including meeting earnings
benchmarks to preserve compensation or job security
\citep{graham2005,healy1999}.  Governance mechanisms such as Big~4 audit
quality, board independence, institutional ownership, and analyst coverage are
theorised to reduce agency costs by constraining opportunistic reporting
\citep{shleifer1997,watts1986}.

Positive Accounting Theory \citep{watts1986} extends this framework by
predicting specific directions of earnings management: managers adopt
income-increasing strategies when approaching debt-covenant thresholds, when
bonus-plan targets are within reach, and when political scrutiny is low.  These
incentive structures interact with detection risk to determine whether a manager
will choose AEM, REM, or a combination of both \citep{graham2005}.

\subsection{Accrual-Based Earnings Management}

AEM exploits discretion in accrual accounting to alter reported earnings
without changing underlying cash flows.  The seminal Jones \citeyearpar{jones1991}
model decomposes total accruals into normal (driven by operating conditions) and
discretionary components, with the latter serving as the manipulation proxy.
Subsequent refinements---the modified Jones model \citep{dechow1995} and the
performance-matched approach \citep{kothari2005}---improved specificity by
controlling for firm performance.  Key AEM indicators include abnormal
accounts-receivable growth, inventory build-up, and divergence between net
income and operating cash flow \citep{richardson2005}.

\subsection{Real Activities Manipulation}

REM alters reported earnings through actions that affect actual cash flows and
operations.  \citet{roychowdhury2006} identified three primary REM channels:
(i)~abnormally high production to spread fixed costs and reduce cost of goods
sold, resulting in inflated inventory; (ii)~sales acceleration through channel
stuffing or price discounts, reducing cash flow from operations relative to
sales; and (iii)~cuts in discretionary expenditures such as R\&D, SG\&A, and
advertising.  \citet{gunny2010} demonstrates that firms engaging in REM to
just meet earnings benchmarks exhibit lower future operating performance,
confirming that REM imposes real economic costs.

\subsection{The AEM--REM Substitution Hypothesis}

The most theoretically important development for the present work is the
documented substitution between AEM and REM.  \citet{cohen2008} documented a
structural break after SOX: AEM declined sharply while REM increased,
consistent with managers switching to harder-to-detect manipulation.
\citet{zang2012} formalised this as a sequential decision model: managers
choose REM first (because it is decided during the fiscal year) and use AEM as
a residual to achieve earnings targets---but only to the extent that audit
quality permits.  \citet{cohen2010} replicated the pattern around seasoned
equity offerings, \citet{badertscher2011} demonstrated that overvalued firms
shift from AEM to REM as accrual manipulation is exhausted, and
\citet{ho2015} and \citet{chi2011} showed that Big~4 audit quality suppresses
AEM but increases REM.

These findings imply that any single-channel detection approach will produce
systematically biased risk scores for firms under high governance pressure.  A
joint detection artifact that captures the \emph{relationship} between AEM and
REM signals is therefore both theoretically motivated and practically necessary.

\subsection{Graph Analytics in Accounting Information Systems}

Graph-based methods have gained traction in financial applications, primarily
in money laundering detection \citep{savage2016}, insider trading networks
\citep{ahern2017}, and supply chain risk analysis.  More recently, graph neural
networks (GNNs) have been applied to fraud detection in transaction networks
\citep{liu2021,wang2019}.  However, these approaches model \emph{inter-entity}
networks, not \emph{intra-entity} financial-statement relationships.  Our
vulnerability graph model is, to our knowledge, among the first to apply graph
analytics to the \emph{internal structure} of financial statements for earnings
manipulation detection, operationalising the relational view of accounting
\citep{dechow2011}.

the six-step process model of \citet{peffers2007}.  DSR is appropriate because

from theory \citep{march1995}.  The three core DSR activities (build, evaluate,
theorise) connect the knowledge base of financial accounting and information

\subsection{Problem Identification and Motivation}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, no existing automated tool jointly
models AEM and REM or detects substitution patterns in real time.  The
economic significance of this gap is confirmed by regulatory enforcement data:
the SEC's AAER database documents over 270 material-misstatement cases between
1982 and 2005, with earnings manipulation the predominant allegation
\citep{dechow2011}.  Existing tools either lack relational modelling or lack
explainability, but not both simultaneously---a design gap that ARS-VG is
specifically engineered to fill.

\subsection{Objectives of a Solution}

The artifact must satisfy five design objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(O1)] Detect AEM signals with precision comparable to state-of-the-art baselines.
    \item[(O2)] Detect REM signals using production, CFO, and discretionary-expenditure anomalies.
    \item[(O3)] Identify and classify AEM--REM substitution patterns.
    \item[(O4)] Provide natural-language explanations for every flagged anomaly.
    \item[(O5)] Allow interactive querying by CIK number via the SEC EDGAR API.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design and Development}

ARS-VG was implemented in Python~3.11, leveraging NetworkX for graph
construction, ChromaDB for vector-store RAG, Ollama/DeepSeek-R1 for LLM
inference, and Gradio for the interactive interface.  The full implementation
is available as a Google Colab notebook to facilitate replication and extension
by other researchers.  Section~\ref{sec:design} details the design decisions.

\subsection{Demonstration}

The artifact was demonstrated on four synthetic but theoretically calibrated
scenarios (Section~\ref{subsec:scenario}): a clean firm, an AEM-dominant
manipulator, an REM-dominant manipulator, and a substitution case with strong
governance.  All four scenarios produced the expected classification outcome
(pass rate = 100\,\%), satisfying design objectives O1--O3.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Utility was evaluated through: (i)~predictive accuracy on the JarFraud dataset
(5-fold stratified cross-validation, Section~\ref{subsec:perf});
(ii)~comparison with two established baselines (Beneish M-Score, Dechow
F-Score); and (iii)~component-level performance analysis quantifying the
marginal contribution of the graph versus the traditional methods.
Ensemble performance is reported in the companion evaluation notebook, which
readers may execute against the full JarFraud panel.

\subsection{Vulnerability Graph Construction}
\label{subsec:graph}

A firm's financial statements are modelled as a directed graph
$G = (V, E)$, where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nodes} $V = V_A \cup V_R \cup V_G$ comprise three types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Account nodes} ($V_A$): revenue, COGS, gross profit,
              operating income, net income, accounts receivable, inventory,
              accounts payable, PP\&E, and total assets.
        \item \emph{Ratio nodes} ($V_R$): DSO, DIO, DPO, and cash-conversion cycle.
        \item \emph{Governance nodes} ($V_G$): auditor type and auditor tenure.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Edges} $E$ connect nodes with known accounting relationships;
    each edge $e_{ij} \in E$ carries an expected ratio $\mu_{ij}$ and standard
    deviation $\sigma_{ij}$ based on industry benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:edges}).
\end{itemize}

For each edge, \emph{edge strain} is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Strain}(e_{ij}) = \frac{|r_{ij} - \mu_{ij}|}{\sigma_{ij}},
\label{eq:strain}
\end{equation}
where $r_{ij}$ is the observed ratio between source and target node values.
Edge strain exceeding 2.0 s.d.\ is classified as \textsc{broken}; between 1.0
and 2.0 as \textsc{stressed}; and below 1.0 as \textsc{intact}.

\subsection{Edge Anomaly Detection with Industry-Adjusted $z$-Scores}
\label{subsec:anomaly}

We apply industry-adjusted $z$-scores to four diagnostically critical edges
that capture both AEM and REM pathways:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (cash quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{CFO}} = \frac{(\text{CFO}/\text{Revenue}) - \mu_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{CFO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where superscript $(k)$ denotes the SIC-based industry group.  A strongly
    negative $z_{\text{CFO}}$ signals revenue that does not convert to
    cash---a hallmark of premature revenue recognition (AEM).

    \item \textbf{Revenue $\to$ Accounts Receivable} (collection quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DSO}} = \frac{\text{DSO} - \mu_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DSO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DSO} = (\text{AR}/\text{Revenue})\times 365$.  Elevated DSO
    suggests receivables growing faster than revenue---a channel-stuffing or
    fictitious-revenue signal.

    \item \textbf{COGS $\to$ Inventory} (production efficiency):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{DIO}} = \frac{\text{DIO} - \mu_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{DIO}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\text{DIO} = (\text{Inventory}/\text{COGS})\times 365$.  Elevated
    DIO signals overproduction to spread fixed costs---a classic REM technique
    \citep{roychowdhury2006}.

    \item \textbf{Net Income $\to$ Operating Cash Flow} (accrual quality):
    \begin{equation}
        z_{\text{Accrual}} = \frac{(\text{NI} - \text{CFO})/\text{TA} - \mu_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}}{\sigma_{\text{Acc}}^{(k)}},
    \end{equation}
    where TA denotes total assets.  A high positive $z_{\text{Accrual}}$
    indicates earnings far above cash generation---a direct AEM indicator.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Graph-Theoretic Risk Metrics}

After computing edge-level anomalies, we derive three graph-theoretic metrics
using NetworkX:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PageRank} \citep{brin1998}: identifies nodes with highest
    importance in the risk-propagation network, weighted by edge strain.
    \item \textbf{Betweenness centrality} \citep{freeman1977}: identifies
    nodes that act as critical intermediaries through which manipulation strain
    must flow.
    \item \textbf{Aggregate node risk}: a weighted combination of adjacent
    edge strain:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Risk}(v)
        = 0.6\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\max_{e \ni v}\,\text{Strain}(e)}{3},\,1\right)
        + 0.4\!\times\!\min\!\!\left(\frac{\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)}{2},\,1\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $\overline{\text{Strain}}(v)$ is the mean strain of edges incident to
    node $v$.
\end{itemize}

The final \textbf{graph risk score} $G$ aggregates edge-level severities:
\begin{equation}
    G
    = \min\!\!\left(1,\;
      0.35\!\times\!n_{\text{broken}}
      + 0.15\!\times\!n_{\text{stressed}}
      + \min\!\!\left(0.5,\;\frac{\overline{|z|}}{4}\right)\right),
    \label{eq:graphrisk}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{broken}}$ and $n_{\text{stressed}}$ count broken and
stressed edges among the four critical edges, and $\overline{|z|}$ is the mean
absolute $z$-score.

\subsection{Traditional Detection Methods}
\label{subsec:traditional}

Two established methods are implemented for comparison and ensemble
integration.

\textbf{Beneish M-Score (simplified) \citep{beneish1999}:}%
\footnote{The original Beneish (1999) model uses eight financial-ratio indices
(DSRI, GMI, AQI, SGI, DEPI, SGAI, LVGI, TATA).  We employ a two-variable
approximation because the JarFraud dataset provides annual (not lagged-year)
observations for a substantial portion of its panel, which prevents computation
of all eight indices without significant sample loss.  The simplified
implementation therefore captures the two dominant predictors (total accruals
and DSO) and is clearly labelled as such throughout.}
\begin{equation}
    M = -4.84 + 4.679\!\times\!\text{TATA} + 0.92\!\times\!\frac{\text{DSO}}{45},
\end{equation}
where $\text{TATA} = (\text{NI} - \text{CFO})/\text{TA}$, transformed to a
probability via $P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-(M+1.78)})$.

\textbf{Dechow F-Score (simplified) \citep{dechow2011}:}%
\footnote{The original \citet{dechow2011} F-Score model includes additional
predictors (e.g., change in cash sales, change in inventory, actual issuance
indicator, and abnormal change in employees).  We employ a three-variable
approximation using the three strongest individual predictors (RSST accruals,
change in receivables, soft-asset ratio) because several auxiliary variables
require data items not uniformly available in the JarFraud panel.  The
simplified implementation is clearly labelled throughout.}
\begin{equation}
    F = -7.893 + 0.790\!\times\!\text{RSST} + 2.518\!\times\!\Delta\text{Rec}
       + 1.979\!\times\!\text{SoftAssets},
\end{equation}
where RSST denotes \citet{richardson2005} accruals, $\Delta\text{Rec}$ is the
change in receivables, and SoftAssets is the soft-asset ratio.  The fraud
probability is $P(\text{fraud}) = 1/(1 + e^{-F})$.

\subsection{Ensemble Method}
\label{subsec:ensemble}

The three detection scores are combined in a weighted ensemble:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Ensemble} = 0.25\!\times\!\hat{B}
                    + 0.35\!\times\!\hat{D}
                    + 0.40\!\times\!G,
    \label{eq:ensemble}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{B}$ is the min-max normalised Beneish probability, $\hat{D}$ is
the Dechow F-Score probability, and $G$ is the graph risk score from
Equation~\eqref{eq:graphrisk}.  The graph component receives the highest weight
(0.40) because it captures relational information absent from the other two
methods; the Dechow F-Score (0.35) is the stronger traditional baseline in
prior literature; the Beneish M-Score (0.25) provides a complementary legacy
signal.  Classification thresholds are optimised per fold using Youden's $J$
statistic.

\subsection{Explainable AI Layer}
\label{subsec:xai}

\textbf{Case retrieval via RAG.}  A vector store (ChromaDB) of historical SEC
enforcement cases is embedded using Sentence-Transformers (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2},
384-dimensional embeddings).  The vector store indexes: (i)~SEC AAER case
descriptions from the JarFraud dataset, and (ii)~academic fraud-pattern
descriptions.  Given a target firm's anomaly profile, the system retrieves the
$k=3$ most similar historical cases by cosine similarity.

\textbf{LLM-based synthesis.}  Detected anomalies, retrieved cases, and
financial context are passed to a locally deployed LLM (DeepSeek-R1 via
Ollama, temperature $= 0.1$) with a structured forensic accounting prompt.
The LLM synthesises a narrative that: (i)~identifies the most likely
manipulation pattern; (ii)~explains which account relationships are strained;
(iii)~compares the pattern to retrieved historical cases; and
(iv)~suggests follow-up audit procedures.

Importantly, the LLM serves a \emph{synthesis} role, not a \emph{detection}
role.  All detection is performed by the deterministic graph and ensemble
layers; the LLM only interprets and communicates findings.  This separation
ensures full reproducibility and auditability of the detection pipeline.

\subsection{AEM--REM Substitution Classification}
\label{subsec:subst}

The governance constraint score $G_C$ mediates the AEM--REM balance.
$G_C$ aggregates five binary or normalised governance proxies:
\begin{equation}
    G_C = \frac{1}{5}\!\left(
      \mathbf{1}_{\text{Big4}}
    + \mathbf{1}_{\text{SOX}}
    + \min\!\left(\frac{\text{InstOwn}}{100},\,1\right)
    + \min\!\left(\frac{\text{BoardInd}}{100},\,1\right)
    + \min\!\left(\frac{\text{AnalystCov}}{20},\,1\right)
    \right),
    \label{eq:gc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}_{\text{Big4}}$ and $\mathbf{1}_{\text{SOX}}$ are indicator
variables for Big~4 auditor and SOX~404 compliance, respectively;
$\text{InstOwn}$ is institutional ownership (\%); $\text{BoardInd}$ is the
percentage of independent directors; and $\text{AnalystCov}$ is the number of
covering analysts (capped at 20).  Higher $G_C$ implies stronger governance,
which agency theory predicts will constrain AEM and redirect manipulation
pressure toward REM \citep{zang2012}.

A substitution pattern (AEM$\to$REM) is flagged when:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{AEM}} > \theta_{\text{AEM}},\quad
    S_{\text{REM}} > \theta_{\text{REM}},\quad
    G_C > \bar{G}_C,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{G}_C$ is the sample median governance score, and thresholds are
set at $\theta_{\text{AEM}} = 0.30$ and $\theta_{\text{REM}} = 0.40$,
corresponding to the 70th percentile of the respective score distributions
across training folds.  This is consistent
with \citeauthor{zang2012}'s \citeyearpar{zang2012} sequential-decision
prediction: high governance makes AEM costly, forcing residual manipulation
through REM.

\section{Empirical Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Dataset}

We use the JarFraud dataset \citep{bao2020}, comprising 146{,}045 firm-year
observations for U.S.\ SEC registrants over 1991--2008, with binary fraud
labels derived from SEC Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Releases
(AAERs).  Of these, 964 firm-years (0.7\,\%) carry confirmed fraud labels,
reflecting the natural base rate among public registrants---consistent
with the widely-cited estimate of ${\sim}$1\,\% annual fraud incidence
\citep{acfe2024}.  Financial variables are sourced from Compustat via the
JarFraud panel.  No industry exclusions are applied; all SIC sectors are
retained to preserve population representativeness.

The graph-enhanced component achieves recall of 75.3\,\%---14.7 percentage

significant ($p < 0.001$, McNemar's paired test; Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar}).

the threshold-sensitivity of the Youden-optimised operating point in the
context of a severely imbalanced dataset (0.7\,\% fraud rate): because the
graph risk score is a bounded aggregate (Equation~\ref{eq:graphrisk}),
its continuous discrimination is compressed relative to parametric
probability models, depressing the global AUC while preserving high recall
at the optimal threshold.  For the intended \emph{screening} use case,
recall is the primary design metric, consistent with the asymmetric cost
of missing a fraud (average shareholder loss per AAER case estimated at
\$18.7M; \citealt{karpoff2008}).

The weighted ensemble achieves the highest recall (77.1\,\%) with
AUC-ROC of 0.553 (95\,\% CI: [0.538, 0.568])---demonstrating that
Dechow's parametric signal and the graph's relational signal provide
complementary fraud evidence.

\textbf{Precision and false-positive burden.}  All methods exhibit low
precision (0.8--1.0\,\%), a direct consequence of the severely imbalanced
base rate (0.7\,\% fraud prevalence).  At the ensemble's operating point,
approximately 125~firms are flagged for every true positive.  This
false-positive rate is consistent with the screening literature
\citep{beneish1999,dechow2011}, where the cost of a missed fraud
(\$18.7M average shareholder loss; \citealt{karpoff2008}) far exceeds
the cost of additional investigative effort.  In practice, ARS-VG is
designed as a \emph{first-pass screening layer}---not a standalone
classifier---that narrows the population for subsequent forensic review.
Improving precision through cost-sensitive learning, active learning,
or analyst-in-the-loop feedback is a priority for future work
(Section~\ref{sec:discuss}).

The simplified Dechow proxy retains the highest AUC-ROC (0.665, 95\,\% CI:
[0.652, 0.677]), consistent with its parametric probability-calibrated
design.  Temporal validation (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}) shows that all
four methods generalise to the held-out 2003--2008 period with ${<\,10\,\%}$
AUC change, confirming robustness beyond the training window.  The simplified Beneish proxy achieves AUC-ROC of 0.571 (95\,\% CI:
[0.550, 0.592]) and recall of 60.6\,\%.  We note that these baseline
results reflect simplified implementations (two and three variables,
respectively) and may understate the performance of the full original
models; direct comparison should therefore be interpreted with this
caveat.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Theoretical Contributions}

\textbf{TC1 --- Joint AEM--REM operationalisation.}  ARS-VG is the first
computational artifact to operationalise the AEM--REM substitution hypothesis
as a real-time, firm-level scoring system.  By modelling both channels
simultaneously in a shared graph, the system detects substitution patterns that
are invisible to single-channel tools, advancing the research agenda called for
by \citet{zang2012} and \citet{cohen2008}.

\textbf{TC2 --- Graph-theoretic earnings management detection.}  The
application of PageRank-based node centrality and path-strain analysis to
financial-statement graphs extends the earnings management detection literature
beyond the accrual-decomposition paradigm.  The graph perspective aligns with
\citet{march1995}'s observation that IS artifacts often produce theoretical
insights unavailable through conventional analytical methods.

\textbf{TC3 --- Governance as a moderator in detection systems.}  By
explicitly incorporating governance constraints (auditor type, institutional
ownership, board independence) into the substitution-classification stage,
ARS-VG operationalises agency-theoretic predictions \citep{jensen1976}
directly in a detection architecture, providing a basis for future empirical
tests of substitution theory across governance regimes.

\subsection{Practical Contributions}

\textbf{PC1 --- Audit planning.}  The high recall of the graph component
makes ARS-VG suitable for first-pass risk screening in large audit engagements,
directing resources to accounts with anomalous graph centrality.

\textbf{PC2 --- Regulatory monitoring.}  The SEC EDGAR integration enables
scalable, CIK-level monitoring across the entire registrant population without
requiring proprietary data access.

\textbf{PC3 --- Explainability.}  The RAG-LLM module generates natural-language
explanations connecting anomalous financial ratios to historical fraud analogues,
supporting auditor judgment and satisfying the explainability requirements
increasingly mandated by regulators \citep{euaiact2024}.

\subsection{Limitations}

Four limitations bound the current findings.  First, the Beneish and Dechow
baselines are simplified implementations (two and three variables,
respectively) due to data constraints in the JarFraud panel; comparison
against the full original models may yield different relative performance.
Second, the JarFraud dataset covers 1991--2008; performance on post-2008
IFRS-influenced reporting has not been tested.  Third, the LLM component
requires GPU-capable infrastructure, limiting zero-resource deployability.
Fourth, precision remains low (0.8--1.0\,\%) due to the severely imbalanced
base rate (0.7\,\% fraud); future work should explore cost-sensitive learning
\citep{craja2020} and active learning to improve specificity without
sacrificing recall.  Additionally, the ensemble weights (0.25, 0.35, 0.40)
and graph risk thresholds are set \textit{a priori}; data-driven optimisation
may improve performance (see Future Research, item~4).

\subsection{Future Research Directions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal graph dynamics.}  Extending to multi-period graphs
    that capture year-over-year changes in edge strain to detect
    \emph{deteriorating} relationship patterns.

    \item \textbf{Graph neural networks.}  Replacing handcrafted graph metrics
    with end-to-end learned GNN representations
    \citep{liu2021,wang2019}.

    \item \textbf{Cross-jurisdictional validation.}  Testing on Chinese listed
    firms, European IFRS filings, and emerging-market registrants to assess
    generalisability \citep{ho2015}.

    \item \textbf{Ensemble weight optimisation.}  Replacing the \textit{a
    priori} weights with data-driven Bayesian optimisation or stacking.

    \item \textbf{Supply-chain network effects.}  Extending the vulnerability
    graph to model multi-firm relationships and manipulation propagation across
    supply chains \citep{savage2016}.
\end{enumerate}

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}
% =============================================================================

This paper presented ARS-VG, a graph-theoretic intelligent artifact designed
through the Design Science Research paradigm to jointly detect AEM and REM
substitution patterns in SEC-registered firms.  Grounded in agency theory and
the AEM--REM substitution hypothesis, ARS-VG operationalises three-stage
anomaly detection (AEM scoring, REM scoring, substitution classification) within
a financial knowledge graph augmented by a RAG-LLM explanation module.

Empirical validation on the JarFraud benchmark ($N = 146{,}045$; 0.7\,\%
fraud rate) demonstrates that the graph component achieves the highest recall
among tested methods (75.3\,\%, McNemar $p < 0.001$ vs.\ simplified baselines), correctly
detects all four canonical manipulation regimes in scenario testing, and the
ensemble combines the complementary strengths of relational and ratio-based
analysis (77.1\,\% recall, AUC-ROC 0.553).  Temporal validation confirms
out-of-sample generalisation across the 2003--2008 hold-out period.  Three
theoretical and three practical contributions extend the earnings management detection literature and provide a reusable
open-source prototype for auditors, regulators, and researchers.

As governance pressure continues to drive the substitution of AEM for REM,
detection systems that model only one channel will become increasingly
inadequate.  ARS-VG offers a principled, theoretically motivated, and
empirically validated response to this evolving challenge.
